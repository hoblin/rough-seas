What moves a hull or a swimmer through the water: the waves, a stroke, the current and a rope. The waves themselves are on [The science of the waves](The-Science-of-the-Waves), and the hull itself on [The science of the hull](The-Science-of-the-Hull).

## How the waves move a hull

The water under a wave moves in a circle: forward under the crest, back in the trough, by `a ω e^(k z)` for a wave of amplitude `a`, frequency `ω` and wavenumber `k`, at `z` under the surface (linear wave theory; Dean and Dalrymple, Water Wave Mechanics, 1991). Over a bed of depth `d` the exponential becomes `cosh k(d + z) / sinh kd`, so the same wave moves the water faster in the shallows. A hull feels the water's pressure summed over its underwater body (the Froude-Krylov force), so the velocity it reads is that one averaged over the body: in deep water `e^(k lift) (1 - e^(-k T)) / (k T)` over its draft `T` under the height it heaves to, and `sinc(k_L L / 2) sinc(k_B B / 2)` over its length `L` and beam `B`, with `k_L` and `k_B` the wave's number along and across its heading.

A hull moves by the sum of the forces on it, over its mass and the water it drags with it (its added mass, Lamb's). The water's pressure and the added mass push it by `(m + A) Du/Dt`, the water's own acceleration (Morison's inertia term), and a floating hull displaces its own mass, so that push gives it exactly the water's acceleration whatever it weighs. So the hull's velocity is the waves' water at its place, `u`, plus the rest, `r`, and only `r` changes by forces. With `v_c` the current and `w` the hull's own way, a rower's, its velocity through the water is `s = r − v_c + w`, and:

```
(m + A) dr/dt = −(m + A) (s·∇)u − D(s) + D(w) + T + F_wind
```

`−(s·∇)u` is the difference between the water's acceleration and the change of `u` along the hull's own path: nothing for a hull lying with the water, and on a wave's front face, where the water ahead runs slower than behind, a push down the face for a hull running with the wave. Near the wave's speed, `s ≈ c`, it is `−c ∂u/∂x = ∂u/∂t`, which in shallow water is `−g ∂η/∂x`: gravity along the face, the force that carries a surfer. `D` is the hull's drag on its velocity through the water ([Driving it](#driving-it)), less `D(w)`, what its own way meets and its rower already pays; `T` a line's tension; `F_wind` the wind's load on what stands above the water and on a set sail ([under sail](The-Science-of-the-Hull#under-sail)). The current's pull is in `D` too, since the hull's velocity through the water counts it. Nothing is written for surf-riding: a hull held on a face near the wave's speed, where the push balances the drag, is carried toward the shore, and a slow hull lets the face pass under it. On the bench a sailboat running at 7 m/s through the water, under vanilla's sail before the wind drove it, before a gale's 5.6 m swell onto a 1:8 beach was pushed on at up to 1.5 m/s² and went from 6.4 to 10.5 m/s over the ground on a face; a canoe paddled at 2.2 m/s let the faces pass.

The hull reads `u` where it is each step, so lying with the water it moves with it as a body in the water does, and each wave carries it a little further forward than back: the Stokes drift, `a^2 ω k` for a small body at the surface (Stokes 1847). Averaging over a hull makes a large one drift slower than a small one. Calvert et al. (2021, JFM 915:A73) measured the opposite effect as well, from a variable submergence that lags the surface; it isn't modelled, because its size hangs on a heave damping we haven't measured.

A hull's own way is measured from vanilla's physics as it moves the hull, not modelled. Each move vanilla scales the motion by its water drag unless a rower takes that off. On the server a move lasts 1/30 s, and vanilla divides a fast motion by up to 1.25 when the move is the tick's only one, not one of ten. On the client that steers the hull a move lasts 1/60 s, and that division never comes.

## A body in the water

A [swimmer](Swimming) is one reference body, a man of 1.75 m (Fryar et al. 2021) and 75 kg, the same crew a vessel counts. Its volume by height comes from segment masses (de Leva 1996), densities (Dempster 1955) and landmarks (Drillis and Contini 1966), and its tissue's volume from its fat by the two-compartment model (Siri 1961). The lungs hold the end of a breath out and half a breath more (Quanjer et al. 1993). That makes 70.9 L of tissue and 3.66 L of air, 74.6 L in all. In seawater, 1025 kg/m³, that displaces 76.4 kg: the body floats with about 1.4 L clear, the water at its face, and has 1.4 kg to spare. In fresh water, 998 kg/m³, it displaces 74.4 kg and sinks slowly. Measured bodies do the same: at the end of a breath out 69% of 98 men floated in seawater and 7% in fresh water (Donoghue and Minnigerode 1977).

The equation up and down, with V what lies under the drawn surface and a and w_w the water's own acceleration and velocity:

```
(m + m_a) dw/dt = ρ g V - m g + (ρ V + m_a) a - ½ ρ Cd A |w - w_w| (w - w_w)
```

This is the linear wave force on a body small against the wave (Journée and Massie 2001, 6.3.3). The water's motion is the drawn surface's own: its rise and its acceleration are the time derivatives of the height drawn, the bed's gain, a broken wave's bore and the reflection included. Under the surface they fade as linear theory has them, sinh k(d + z) / sinh kd, e^(kz) in deep water, with the profile stretched over the water column up to the drawn surface (Wheeler 1970), so the surface moves exactly as drawn and the water stands still at the bed; the body reads them at the middle of its part under water. Across, the same form takes the water's horizontal motion, the one a hull takes ([How the waves move a hull](#how-the-waves-move-a-hull)): the change of the water's velocity at the body and `−(s·∇)u` for its own velocity `s` through the water, each times `(ρ V + m_a) / (m + m_a)`, which is less than one for a body that doesn't displace its whole mass.

The water the body lies in is the waves' and the current's, so a current carries a swimmer by the same drag. The swimmer's own stroke is a thrust, as a rower's is: 38.9 N the way the movement keys point from where the swimmer looks, up while jump is held. That is the drag triathletes, the trained swimmers nearest an ordinary one measured, swam against at their 0.90 m/s, 35 W of it (Toussaint 1990, *Med. Sci. Sports Exerc.* 22(3)); at rest, tethered, trained swimmers pull more, 92 to 104 N on average (Neiva et al. 2011). A swimmer swimming across lies along the stroke, so its drag then is that of the same front crawl, Cd A = 2 D / (ρ v²) = 0.096 m², and it reaches about 0.9 m/s. Treading water or still, it stays upright and drags broadside.

Added mass is the hulls' own model (Lamb, *Hydrodynamics*, §373). While the surface cuts the body, it is Lamb's disc of the body's section at the waterline, the free surface carrying half (Newman, *Marine Hydrodynamics*): (4/3) ρ a³ for a round one, about 11 kg with the chest at the water (a = 0.20 m) and half a kilogram with only the head out. Once the body is wholly under, it is Lamb's prolate spheroid of its own length and volume: 0.044 of its displaced water along its length, 3.3 kg, up and down, and 0.92 of it across, 70 kg, taken across by the share of the body under water. The drag areas are 0.25 m² along the body and 0.45 m² broadside, from skydivers' fall speeds (FAI); nothing was found measured for a body moving up and down in water.

Held air shrinks by Boyle's law at the chest's depth, lungs × p₀ / (p₀ + ρ g d). The body turns negative once its 3.66 L of air are squeezed to 2.26 L, with the chest about 6 m down, and sinks from there.

What a player carries follows the game's line: armour, and what the hotbar's items call a tool or weapon by their own type. Full plate in the game slows a walker by 0.42, and tested field armour weighs 35 ± 5 kg (Askew et al. 2012), so armour weighs 83 kg to the unit of that penalty. Tools are by real analogues, listed in `config/person.json`, all taken as steel, which keeps 87% of its weight in seawater: a knife (0.7 kg) floats the body lower, an axe (1.85 kg) outweighs its 1.4 kg to spare.

A crest breaks over a swimmer by the vessels' rule: a wave passes each dominant period, its height drawn from the Rayleigh spread; in surf it breaks for certain when it is among the tallest the breaking share cuts off ([Over the bed](The-Science-of-the-Waves#over-the-bed)), and elsewhere by the wind's whitecap share ([Breaking](The-Science-of-the-Waves#waves)). Its plume holds a mean of about 20% air just after breaking and 1% after one wave period (Lamarre and Melville 1994), not the 24% peak measured at one gauge 0.2 m down at sea (Lamarre and Melville 1992). It reaches 0.6 wave heights down, twice the depth of the air's centre after a period (Lamarre and Melville 1994). Echosounders find bubbles far deeper (Derakhti et al. 2024), too dilute to matter to a body. Its roller runs at 0.75 of the breaker's speed (Rapp and Melville 1990) for about a second (Callaghan 2013).

## Driving it

A rowed, paddled or poled hull goes where its rower's thrust meets the water's drag on it, step by step: its speed changes by the difference over its mass and the water it drags with it, and its turn by the difference of the torques over the hull's and the water's moment about its middle. Nothing sets its speed but that.

- **The water's drag along the hull** is three parts. Friction: the ITTC-1957 line, 0.075 / (log₁₀ Re − 2)², on its wetted surface by Mumford's formula, 1.7 L T + V/T, times a form factor of 1.2 for a slender round-bilged small craft (Molland, Turnock and Hudson 2011), in seawater of 1.19·10⁻⁶ m²/s (ITTC 2011); below Re 5·10⁵ the flow stays laminar and is read at that transition. A blunt bow's own drag, its coefficient on the hull's beam by its draft: the raft's sawn log ends 1.0 (Hoerner 1965). Across the raft's 1.32 m of logs, awash to their full 0.25 m, they take 43 of the 47 N it meets at half a metre a second, nearly all a paddler gives: that, not its weight, is what makes a raft a barge. And the waves it makes: Michell's thin-ship integral (Michell 1898; Tuck 1989) over a hull of its length, beam and draft, parabolic along it and down it, the Wigley form the theory is checked against in tow tanks; it reproduces the Wigley hull's own curve, with its hump and hollow. Checked against a racing kayak towed on a sprint course (Gomes et al. 2015), the law gives 28 N where it pulled 21 at 2.8 m/s, and 102 where it pulled 89 at 5.6 m/s: within a third, and on the heavy side, as Michell's theory is near its hump.
- **A hull drags water with it**: Lamb's prolate spheroid of its length and beam along it, and across it a flat plate of its draft (Newman 1977).
- **Turning**, each strip of the hull's length meets the water across it with a cross-flow drag coefficient of 0.6 to 1.0 for a round-bilged section, 0.75 taken (Faltinsen 1990; Hoerner 1965), on its length by its draft and the outrigger's float. Its moment is that of a rod of its length. A short, round hull meets almost no water as it turns: the coracle spins. Under way its underwater body also lifts against the turn, in proportion to its speed: the linear yaw damping of Clarke, Gedling and Hine's regression over ship models (Trans. RINA 1983), −π (T/L)² (0.25 + 0.039 B/T − 0.56 B/L) in the usual non-dimensional form, whose first term is slender-body theory's. It is what makes a hull under way run straight and turn on a wider circle: paddled round, the bark canoe turns on some 7.5 m at speed.
- **The rower** is the average finisher of a canoe marathon, some 30 km in two hours: a trained club paddler. Young male flatwater kayakers reach 138.5 W at their aerobic maximum (Frontiers in Public Health 2020), and two hours hold about 75% of it, between a marathon's 74% and a half marathon's 81% (fractional utilisation of marathon and ultramarathon runners): 104 W at the paddle. Through a paddle into a racing K1, whose drag Gomes et al. measured, it holds it at about 10.7 km/h, a club marathon pace. His body gives 1040 W for it at paddling's gross efficiency of 10% (Gomes et al. 2012); that is the limit, and each stroke turns its own share of it into work.
- **A blade** works by slipping through the water: pulled back past the hull, it pushes on the water at its drag, ½ρ C_d A s², its slip s squared, for the share of the stroke it is in the water. The rower spends that force times the blade's speed past the hull, the hull's speed plus the blade's slip, so a faster hull or a smaller blade loses more to slip. C_d is a flat plate's 1.2 (Hoerner 1965), which a paddle blade keeps across designs (Sports Engineering, BF02844156).
- **The paddle**, 1.5 m with a blade 54 by 10 cm, is the bark canoe's, the outrigger's and the coracle's paddles of that size and width (WHS maple paddle, c. 1890; Peabody Museum; Science Museum Group co42017). Along the side it is in the water half the stroke, at 40 strokes a minute, the voyageurs' pace (McGill University, *The Birch Bark Canoe*). Sculled over the coracle's bow it drives the whole sweep, turning only at its ends.
- **One side.** A single blade drives the hull from its side, as far out from its middle as the stroke reaches, so it turns the hull away from that side as it drives it. A paddler holding a course changes sides once the hull has veered off it, as the Donegal curragh's paddler does and canoe and outrigger crews do on a call. Nobody has measured how far off they let it go, but how often they change has been counted: 3 to 5 strokes a side in a touring canoe, 8 to 20 in an outrigger (Gold Coast Outrigger Club; Journal of Sports Science and Medicine 2011). At 10° off, our bark canoe and outrigger change about that often. The change takes a stroke without drive, the paddle carried over the bow while the hands change grips.
- **The raft's paddler** kneels by the side he paddles and shuffles across its logs when he changes sides, his trunk bent 15° toward his paddle, well within its 25° (AAOS). No source shows a raft paddled; from its middle no arm reaches water past 0.67 m of logs, and kneeling by its side, as a canoeist kneels to paddle, puts the blade 0.81 m out from its middle, clear of them.
- **The oars** of the currach are 3 m, with blades 1 m by 10 cm, almost none, as Aran oars are (Royal Museums Greenwich; Hornell 1938). On a thole pin a rower pulls with the whole body, and rowing turns 17.5% of his effort into work (Fukunaga et al. 1986): from the same 1040 W, about 180 W, against a paddler's 104. He sits facing its stern, his thwart where the currach floats level with him and his oars aboard, 0.12 m forward of its middle, since no source places a lone rower's thwart; the pins stand 0.38 m abaft it, as at Dingle (Hornell gives none for Aran), and each loom reaches from its pin to the hull's middle, Hornell's rule for the inboard, so the grips cross by up to 0.23 m (the Celtic MKE currach club: 6 to 12 in). He draws the grips to his lower ribs, as rowers do, which dips each oar until 0.057 m² of its upright blade is under the water. The stroke sweeps 50° forward of square at the catch, as fixed-seat rowers do (gigrower.co.uk; John Welsford), and aft until the grips reach his ribs, 27° past square. He swings from the hips, from 22.5° forward to 25° back, as the world's best rowers do (Kleshnev), his arms straight while his back can carry the stroke: arms then trunk on the recovery, trunk then arms in the drive. Two blades, 4.12 m apart across the hull as they sweep, drive it at 30 strokes a minute (the Annapolis currach races), two fifths of it in the water. Turning, one is pulled and the other backed.
- **A pole** pushes off the bottom without slipping, at a sustained two-handed push of 300 N (Snook and Ciriello 1991), while the hands travel its planted length, some 2 m, then it is brought up hand over hand for as long again (Nicolson 2008, *Basic Punting for Darwinians*), 15 strokes a minute. It drives the boat only while it is on the bed, and the boat glides while the pole comes up, so it surges with each shove and gathers way over several. Below a metre a second the shove takes the 2 s it is drawn in; faster, the boat runs past the pole's 2 m sooner, the shove ends sooner, and the poler brings the pole up and plants again sooner, as Nicolson's punter does: his beat quickens with the boat, and his shove is drawn as quick. A pole too short for the water ends the shove sooner still, when its top reaches the upper hand, so at the very limit of its reach it has nothing left to push.
- **Bringing it up** costs the work of lifting it from the bed until its foot reaches the surface, less what the water bears of it while it is still under (Archimedes); once a shove's push is spent on that, nothing is left to drive the boat. The pole lifted is one made for pushing a boat, round and 5 cm across, as ash boat poles of 35 to 46 mm are, at its wood's density, however thick the pole was dressed. It is paid once a stroke, whether the shove drives the boat, turns it or both.
- **Its reach.** A pole is planted at 20° from upright and reaches the bottom through its length, cos 20° of it, less the height of the rower's hands over the water. Going ahead it is planted astern of his hands and left behind as the boat passes it; going astern it is planted ahead of them and shoved toward the bow. The bed is sounded where the pole's foot lands, and the drawn pole slides through his hands to stand on it, as far as its top end in his upper hand.
- **The poler stands** wherever the hull bears a man standing, as canoe polers and punters do (ACA, *Canoe Poling*; Paddle Canada, *Introduction to Canoe Poling Skills*; Nicolson 2008). Whether it does is its initial metacentric height with a 75 kg man standing on its floor, his centre of mass 0.98 m over his soles (0.557 of a 1.756 m stature: de Leva 1996 segment masses at Drillis and Contini 1966 heights; ANSUR II, Gordon et al. 2014): KB + BM − KG, the waterplane an ellipse of its area and the hull's beam, a rectangle for the raft and the reed boat's bundles, with the outrigger's float at its distance. A hull bears him if it is at least as stiff as the narrowest canoe polers still stand in, about 28 inches (0.71 m) wide; narrower, they kneel. Taken as the bark canoe at that width, that is −0.16 m, a canoe a standing man must balance. The bark canoe gives −0.07 m, the coracle +0.03, the dugout +0.12, the currach +1.3, the outrigger and the raft far more. The reed boat's two bundles, 0.39 m across, give −0.53, and he poles it from the seat.
- **Where he stands:** in the bark canoe a foot behind its centre thwart, in the diagonal stance on the side he poles (ACA; Paddle Canada); in the dugout at the end he faces, as dugouts were poled from either end (Florida Museum) and the mokoro and the pirogue from an end; in the outrigger, whose poler no source places, where its dugout hull's stands; in the currach, which no source shows poled, a foot aft of its middle, as in a canoe; the coracle in its middle; the raft on its logs half a metre toward the side he poles, so his pole meets the water past them. He plants the pole close by the hull behind his rear foot, drives by bending his knees and turning his trunk, his hands walking up the pole to its top end, and draws it forward through his hands to plant again (Paddle Canada). His hands end the shove at his hip, 0.93 m over his soles (0.530 of his stature, Drillis and Contini).
- **Standing, he drives with his legs too.** Poling has not been measured; the nearest measured stroke is the stand-up paddler's, 13.4% gross at 45 strokes a minute, its slowest rate and the nearest to poling's 15 (Castañeda-Babarro et al. 2020); a gondolier rowing standing spends 9.2 to 14.5% (Capelli et al. 1990). So a standing poler turns 13.4% of the rower's 1040 W into work, 139 W. From the seat he shoves with arms and trunk at paddling's 10%, his hands at his chest as his stroke draws them, 0.49 m above the water on the reed boat.
- **The effort** costs satiety on vanilla's own scale: vanilla charges 1.5 satiety a second for sprinting, which a 75 kg runner at its 3.4 m/s spends 1.07 kW on (Margaria 1963, 4.2 J a kilogram and metre), so a kilojoule of effort costs 1.4 satiety, scaled with the calendar as vanilla scales hunger.

## Carried by the current

- **The drag.** A current moves a hull apart from the water only while the two differ, and the force is the drag the hull is driven against when rowed, on its whole velocity through the water, its own way and what the water has given it together, less the share its way alone meets, which its rower already pays: along the hull the friction, the blunt bow's drag and the waves it makes, across it the cross-flow drag on length by draft and any keel or float (Hoerner's cross-flow principle, Hoerner 1965, *Fluid-Dynamic Drag*).
- **Its answer.** The hull takes that force with its mass and the water it drags with it: Lamb's spheroid along it, a flat plate of its draft across it (Lamb 1932, *Hydrodynamics*, §114; Newman 1977, *Marine Hydrodynamics*). A drag that grows with the square of the speed takes the water's speed off it as w / (1 + k w t), so it comes up to the current quickly at first and ever more slowly after.
- **What it comes to.** At 2 m/s the water pulls 44 times harder than at 0.3 m/s. An empty oak dugout, 252 kg, lying along a 1.5 m/s stream makes 0.49 m/s of it after 10 s and 0.89 m/s after 30 s; with 400 kg aboard, 0.33 and 0.67 m/s. Lying across it, both make about 1.3 m/s after 10 s. The sailboat lying along it makes only 0.57 m/s after 30 s.
- **On a mooring.** Held at the bow, the water across the hull turns it about the bow, each strip of its side meeting the water less its own swing, about the moment of a rod at its end, until the water runs along it, stern downstream. There it pulls least: at 1.5 m/s, 1900 N on a sailboat lying across and 70 N lying along.

## Held by a rope

- **A line only pulls.** A stake rope or an anchor's rode is a line of its own length L, slack within it. At its end it stretches like a spring, T = k x with k = EA / L: natural-fibre rope stretches 10% at its breaking load (machovec.com, rope comparison), so EA is the breaking load over 0.1, 110 kN for flax at 11 kN (BS EN ISO 1181). A longer line is softer.
- **The steady pull.** The current's drag on the hull and the waves' push on it, (m + added mass) times the water's acceleration where it is held ([How the waves move a hull](#how-the-waves-move-a-hull)), add as vectors; a single taut line carries the part of that pull along it, and two taut lines share it so their tensions, each along its own line, add up to it. A line the balance would push goes slack. A line at a slope, as a rode rising from its anchor, carries its share over the cosine of its slope.
- **The jerk.** The line's tension is one more force on the hull, and nothing else stops it: no velocity is cut. A line is stiff against a light hull, and its own period can be shorter than a tick, so each tick is solved as the time it is: the hull runs free until it reaches the line's end, the line stretches as a spring on its mass and added mass along it, m, with the steady pull F along it, m x'' = F − k x, lets it go, and takes it up again as often as the tick lasts. The hull is moved as far as that solution takes it, so one long tick, a lagging server's, leaves it where many short ones would. A hull reaching a line's end at speed v stretches it until the line has taken its energy, T = F + √(F² + k m v²), then springs it back; the bounce dies in the water's drag and the hull lies at F. The highest tension within each tick is the one the line is judged by. A sailboat of 2.4 t surged out at 1.6 m/s parts a 2.5 m flax rope (about 16 kN) and not a 9 m one (about 9 kN). A raft of 135 kg coming taut on a 5.5 m rope at 0.60 m/s jerks it at 1048 N, against 1046 N from the formula, and lies at 40 to 100 N in a light swell.
- **The stone anchor.** It holds by friction on the bed: 37% of its dry weight on sand and 49% on flat rock, measured by dragging Bronze Age one-hole stone anchors under water (Miller, Cvikel & Me-Bar 2023, *J. Archaeol. Sci. Reports* 52, 104284). A rode rising from it at angle θ lifts it: it holds T = μ W' / (cos θ + μ sin θ), with W' its weight under water (granite, 2650 kg/m³). Our 100 kg stone holds about 330 N on sand and 420 N on rock at a scope of five. Pulled past that it slides, and the vessel drifts with it, held back by that holding force along the rode and never drawn back by it; a stake rope tied beside it takes the rest of the pull. A rope of a material we don't know never parts and stops the vessel at its end.
