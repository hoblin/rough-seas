What Rough Seas' hulls stand on: how they take a blow, float, leak and ride the swell, and where we bent it. The sea that strikes them is on [The science of the sea](The-Science-of-the-Sea).

## Knocks

- **Water in the bilge sloshes.** Water with a free surface doesn't stop with the hull: it runs on, piles against the bow a moment later and spends part of its energy in the splash. A hull brimful moves almost as one body, so the mod counts the water's weight by how full the bilge is. The part left free to slosh then peaks at half full, which is the [free surface effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface_effect) naval architects guard against.
- **Armour's weight comes from its stride.** The game gives armour no weight, only a slower walk. The mod reads the weight back from that penalty, calibrated on plate: a complete field harness of the fifteenth century weighed 15 to 25 kg (Alan Williams, *The Knight and the Blast Furnace*, 2003), and the game's plate slows its wearer by 0.14 a piece. Another mod's armour weighs by its own penalty.
- **Rams.** Two hulls that meet lose the energy of their closing speed held by their reduced mass, ½ μ v², μ = m₁m₂ / (m₁ + m₂), as in any inelastic collision; each hull takes half.

## Hulls

- **A knock, a ram, rapids or lightning** loads the whole frame: its energy is weighed against the hull's capacity, and cracks its place by that share. A hull's capacity scales by its wood's modulus of rupture against oak's, from the [Wood Database](https://www.wood-database.com/): ebony 158 MPa, purpleheart 152, bamboo 130, birch 114, maple 109, walnut 101, oak 97, larch 90, acacia 87, pine 83, bald cypress 73, redwood 62, aged wood 49, kapok 36. A species with no published value counts as 93.5, the middle of the vanilla woods.
- **A blow on the shell** breaks it around a hole: its toughness times the hole's rim times the shell's thickness. Woods' toughness is their impact bending work at 12% moisture (Kollmann et al., in the Norwegian handbook *Wood – nature's most beautiful raw materials*, 1999): birch 94 kJ/m², beech 83, oak 75, ash 68, maple 65, elm 60, linden 50, pine and spruce 40; a wood not in the table counts as 60, their median. Willow takes aspen's, of its own family and weight, and thuja spruce's and pine's. In oak, a sailboat's 19 mm plank gives to about 250 J, a dugout's 5 cm wall to about 670 J, and the bark canoe's bark with its lining of oak laths to about 43 J. A weapon strikes with 200 J a point of its damage, a blast or a bolt with 1000 J a point.
- **A cut** spends its work on parting the fibres alone: softwood's work of fracture across the grain is 9.2 kJ/m² (*Nature* 252, 116, 1974) against 40 kJ/m² of impact bending work, 0.23 of it. Hide tears at 17 kJ/m² (pig skin by trouser tear, Comley and Fleck 2010); a lashing, unmeasured as rope, as flax in epoxy, 5.7 kJ/m². Bark splits along its grain as a thick birch veneer, at birch's work of fracture, 656 J/m² (*Wood Science and Technology*, 2024).
- **No two pieces of wood are alike.** Clear wood scatters around its table value with a coefficient of variation of 16% in bending strength, 25% in impact bending and 34% in toughness (Wood Handbook, FPL-GTR-282, table 5-6), lognormally; every blow meets its own piece.

## Breakers

- **A breaker's pressure.** A spilling roller presses as a stream does, ½ρc². A plunging crest slams a flat panel at 1.2 times its speed with a coefficient of 2π: ½ρ·2π·(1.2c)² (DNV-RP-C205, 2010, §8.7.2.5 and §8.8.1.2). In deep water a spilling breaker presses about 6 kPa at 1 m high, 23 kPa at 4 m and 56 kPa at 10 m, a plunging one nine times that. Which breakers spill and which plunge: [Waves](The-Science-of-the-Sea#waves).
- **It bends the planking.** A strip under an even pressure breaks at its modulus of rupture times thickness² over 6 k span², k being 1/12 for a plank fastened at both ends, 1/8 for a lath propped at its ends and 1/2 for a dugout's side standing free from the bottom. The sailboat's planks are 19 mm on frames 24 cm apart, as Herreshoff's rules (1927) give a yacht her size. Wet wood bends to 0.57 of its dry strength, the mean over eleven woods (Wood Handbook). In oak, the coracle's laths give at 21 kPa, the currach's at 32, the bark canoe at 70, the sides of the dugout, and of the outrigger built on one, at 164, and the sailboat's planks at 705; the raft's logs at about 3 MPa.

## Floating

- **Archimedes.** A hull sinks until it pushes aside its own weight of seawater, 1025 kg/m³, so her draft is her full mass over 1025 times her waterplane, the area she cuts at the water. Each vessel's depth and waterplane come from its real form: the dugout a 4.5 m log 0.75 m wide with rounded ends, the currach a 4 m hull 1.1 m wide and full amidships. A raft and a reed boat are solid, pressed down through their own thickness.
- **What each material weighs.** Woods by their average dried weight in the [Wood Database](https://www.wood-database.com/): oak 675 kg/m³, pine 550, ebony 955, ekki 1065; kapok by CIRAD's Tropix, red mangrove by Galvão et al. (2025). Hide counts as dry leather, 860 kg/m³, and cast-iron ballast 7200 ([Engineering ToolBox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/density-solids-d_1265.html)); birch bark 560 (*Silva Fennica*, article 5113). A wood of no known species weighs 610, the middle of the vanilla woods. A hull's weight is given for her reference build, oak for a wooden one, and the same shape in another wood weighs by its density.
- **Ballast.** A keelboat carries about two fifths of her weight as ballast to stand up to her sail, so the sailboat does, in iron, and an oak one flooded goes down.
- **A body floats awash.** A human body is about as dense as seawater, so the water holds up the people aboard a flooded hull; their gear, their armour and the containers weigh on it.
- **Speed.** At the same paddling power, drag grows with the wetted area, which grows as displacement to the ⅔, and with speed squared: speed goes as displacement to the −2/9. A hull's speed follows her full mass against her reference build with one 75 kg rider.

## Leaks

- **Torricelli's law.** Water runs in through a hole at √(2 g h), h the depth of the hole under the waterline. Through a sharp-edged hole the jet narrows, and the flow is 0.61 of the hole's area times that speed: a contraction of 0.62 by a velocity coefficient of 0.98 (US Bureau of Reclamation, *Water Measurement Manual*, chapter 2).
- **Against a real table.** A hull stove in at one place leaks like a 5.6 cm hole: three litres a second 20 cm down. A hard knock's crack, a quarter of that, lets in 56 litres a minute a foot down, a little over what [Landfall Navigation](https://landfallnavigation.com/)'s flooding table gives a 2.5 cm hole there: 45 litres a minute. A crack under the water already inside her leaks by the difference between the sea outside and the water in her, so a bottom crack lets in as much while she fills as when she was dry.
- **Where a side cracks.** Rocks and quays meet a side at the turn of the bilge, a quarter of the way up from the keel: above the water in a light boat, below it once she is laden.
- **Reed.** Bound into bundles, dry totora weighs 270 kg/m³: a Huanchaco *caballito de totora* 2.44 × 0.41 × 0.23 m weighs 41 kg dry (Smithsonian Folklife Festival), and the reed boat, 3 × 0.4 × 0.2 m of the same build, 43 kg in her bundles' 160 litres. A game reed weighs about 0.15 kg, from the rush mat: one square metre of it from 8 reeds, as a real one weighs 1 to 1.5 kg; so her bundles take 288 reeds, and a rope's six reeds make a cord of 90 g a metre, as a real 1 cm one is. A bundle of reed soaks: the caballito weighs some 27 kg more after a day in the sea, so reed takes up two thirds of its weight in water a day afloat; on Lake Titicaca the totora *balsas* are dried ashore between trips and last months, and Heyerdahl's papyrus *Ra II*, with nowhere to dry, settled about 10 cm a day crossing the Atlantic.
- **Her ends sag as she soaks.** They stand up only by the stiffness of their bound reed against their own weight; soaked, the reed is heavier and limp. Nothing holds them up, as nothing held up the stern of Heyerdahl's first *Ra*. How far they sag goes straight with how near to saturated the reed is, drawn at four steps; she sinks before her ends lie flat.

## Riding the swell

- **Heave and pitch.** A hull bobs on a natural period of 2π √((M + A) / (ρ g A_w)), its mass M and the water A it drags with it, about its own mass for a small open boat (Aalto University's seakeeping notes). For a hull of straight sides that is 2π √(2 × draft / g): about 0.6 s for the bark canoe, 1.3 s for an oak dugout and 2.3 s for the sailboat.
- **Roll.** The US Coast Guard's rule for small fishing vessels, 46 CFR 28.575, gives the roll period as 2 C B / √GM, with C = 0.373 + 0.023 B/d − 0.00043 L from her beam B, draft d and length L; GM is that of a box hull with her centre of gravity at half her depth. A small hull without bilge keels rolls on for several swings, at a damping of 0.06 to 0.1 of critical; ours rolls at 0.1.
- So a light canoe follows a long swell as the water lies, while a heavy, deep hull lets a short chop pass under her and rolls on its own longer period.

## Building a dugout

- **Fire, then the adze.** Where people had no metal, they hollowed a log by fire: Thomas Harriot saw the Algonquians of Roanoke make their canoes "only with the helpe of fire, harchets of stones, and shels" (*A Briefe and True Report*, 1588), and John White drew them at it, a fire burning along the log while others scrape. Burnt wood comes away far more easily than sound wood (the Butser Prometheus project, [EXARC](https://exarc.net/ark:/88735/10176)), so each fire is followed by scraping out its char down to sound wood.
- **How fast it burns.** A fire of three to five hours chars 6 to 13 mm into the log, and wet clay packed along the upper edges keeps it from burning through the sides ([Agency House](https://www.agencyhouse.org/dugout-canoe), 2021–2023). Builders lay the clay again each morning before the day's fire (the [PHMC dugout project](https://pahistoricpreservation.com/experimental-archaeology-the-historical-and-museum-commission-building-dugout-canoe/), 2005), and Wampanoag builders, keeping the fire going round the clock, burn out a mishoon in 6 to 10 days ([Plimoth Patuxet](https://plimoth.org/blog/the-making-of-a-mishoon)). The mod chars 2.5 mm an hour, so each of the six fires that hollow out the dugout's 0.49 m burns about 33 hours, a day and a night, about a week in all.
- **The trunk the logs make.** Five logs lying end to end are a trunk 5 m long and 1 m across: the blank is that trunk where it lay, 3927 litres, two and a half tonnes of oak. Builders debarked the log, hewed its top flat and shaped its outside as they burnt it out (a Haida dugout begun as a squared log; the Wampanoag mishoon, flat on top under its fire). Each of her six hewings takes a sixth of the 2236 litres outside the finished hull with the char: first the bark, then the top down to a level sheer 0.56 m above the keel, her bottom round and her ends raked, until she is 4.5 m long and 0.9 m wide (de Bry's engraving, the Timucuan and Carpow logboats).
- **Clay by the game's own arithmetic.** In vanilla's clay forming a ball of clay lays 25 voxels. Wet clay one voxel thick over the rim and the solid ends the model draws covers 608 voxels on the fresh trunk and 293 on the last fire's, as she narrows: 25 balls to 12.
- **An unguarded rim burns through.** With no clay on it, the rim chars at the same 2.5 mm an hour as the hollow. Her walls are 5 cm, so 20 hours of a fire without clay char right through them; a full fire is 33, and the blank is spoiled.
- **Char crumbles.** Burnt wood has lost its fibres, so an axe doesn't have to cut it: a blow breaks it away down to sound wood wherever the edge sweeps. One stroke across the 0.75 m hollow, under a 10 cm edge, takes out a strip of the day's 8 cm of char, about 6 litres, and a hewing's 193 litres take 32 strokes. A woodsman's best rhythm with an axe is 25 to 35 strokes a minute (H. Glaser, physiology of forestry work, [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/X5373E/x5373e04.htm)), a stroke every 2 seconds. About a minute a hewing. The sound wood she loses outside at each hewing, 373 litres, is shaped with the same strokes: really two and a half hours of adze work, it is left untimed so a hewing stays a minute's hold.
- **Rain puts it out.** Rain douses the fire as it douses any of the game's fires, and no fire burns in a hollow with water in it.
- **The blank weighs.** Until it is cut out and hewn away, the wood is there: a fresh oak blank is the round trunk, 2651 kg, and floats 0.66 m deep as a 1 m log does, against the finished dugout's 361 kg, 535 litres of wood around a 1156-litre hold. Her depth and waterplane go from the trunk's to the hull's a sixth a hewing. A blank of a wood heavier than water sinks.
- **The uncut wood backs her.** Below the cut, a blow on her side or bottom meets the solid wood behind the 5 cm wall as well as the wall, and resists by the thickness of both: a copper axe that would stave in a finished side only cracks a fresh blank.
- **Launching.** A person pushes about 300 N steadily (Snook and Ciriello, *Ergonomics* 34, 1991), and wood slides over wet sand or mud at a friction of about 0.4. One push carries that force a step, 0.6 m, so it slides the vessel as far as that work moves her weight against the friction: 12 cm for an oak dugout, under 2 cm for a fresh blank.

## Building a bark canoe

- **One sheet, no saw.** The peoples of the northern woodlands built their canoes with stone and bone tools, no saw: bark peeled from a standing birch, a frame split from cedar with wedges, lashings of spruce root and seams of spruce gum (Adney and Chapelle, *The Bark Canoes and Skin Boats of North America*, 1964). In the game the bark comes off in one sheet, which is the canoe from the start; vanilla rope stands in for the root and vanilla's pitch glue, resin cooked with charcoal, for the gum.
- **Every held action is real time over sixty.** A game day passes in 48 real minutes, 30 times faster than the real one, and boat work is halved again, so that an hour of real work is a minute held. Each action is one piece of work. Peeling a five-metre sheet takes some 20 to 40 minutes: half a minute. A row of wedges splits a trunk in some 15 to 20, about as long as felling such a tree with an axe: 18 seconds. A steamed rib has to be driven home within a couple of minutes, before it cools ([Robin Wood](https://www.robin-wood.co.uk/wood-craft-blog/2014/09/28/birch-bark-canoe-build-final-chapter/), 2014): three seconds a lath. Ten metres of lashing, some fifty turns through holes made with an awl, take about a quarter of an hour: 15 seconds a rope. Only the rib is measured; the rest are estimates.
- **Laths split from poles.** The model draws 29 laths: an inwale and an outwale on each side and three thwarts, a stem at each end, three strakes of sheathing on each side and fourteen ribs. Each names the length it takes, measured off the model along its run: her gunwales are 4.4 m, so 5 m laths, and her sheathing 3.9 m, so 4 m ones; her ribs 1 to 1.2 m, her thwarts 0.7 m and her stems 0.9 m, so any lath of 2 m or more. A piece takes any lath at least as long, cut down, the offcut lost. A pole, some 11 cm square, halved four times along its grain gives 16 laths of about 1 by 6 cm, a canoe rib's size (⅜ by 2½ to 3 inches, Adney and Chapelle): two poles frame a canoe.
- **Lashing by its length.** The Malecite lashed bark to gunwale in groups of eight to ten turns of root, about two inches apart (Adney and Chapelle). Around two gunwales of 1¼ inch and the bark between them, a turn is 20 cm of root, so each metre of gunwale takes 23 m, and the canoe 210 m with its stems: 22 ropes, at the 10 m a vanilla rope pays out.
- **Pitch by the pot.** A bark canoe takes about a quart of pitch (WCHA forum, [bark pitch questions](https://forums.wcha.org/threads/bark-pitch-questions.6357/latest)): a litre, the pot of glue vanilla cooks from two resin and two charcoal powder, a quarter litre on each of her four seams.
- **Wet laths, set ribs.** Ribs were soaked or scalded, bent in, and left to set; bent ribs set in a couple of days (the [Lac du Flambeau build](https://canoe.csumc.wisc.edu/LdFCanoe_subpage_Construction_chronological.html)). Water enters a thin lath by the same diffusion it leaves by, so a lath soaks through in a barrel in as long as it takes to dry: two days each way, at 0.08 litres a metre of lath, about a third of its dry weight, from air-dry past the fibre saturation point. She is finished when her last rib has set.
- **What she is as she grows.** The bark sheet alone is half of her 30 kg cedar build. Her laths add their share as they go in, the volume cedar would have at the weight of the wood split: a frame of oak makes her 44 kg, of birch 42. A knock is taken by as much of her build as is laid, in the strength of her laths' wood against cedar's: a bare sheet takes half the finished cedar canoe's 2400 J, a finished oak one 5200. Her sheathing backs the bark and bends against a breaker only where it is laid, and her hold holds water only once the seams are pitched.

## Building an outrigger

- **A dugout built onward.** Austronesian outriggers were built without metal or saw: a burnt and hewn dugout, a float of a smaller log shaped with an adze and pointed at its ends, two or three booms across the hull lashed with fibre cord to the gunwales and the float, and a crab-claw sail of matting on two spars, with a lashed mast and cord stays. The rig went onto a finished hull, and was sometimes moved from one hull to another. In the game the hull is the dugout's own, with its cracks and its bilge; vanilla rope stands in for the cord and vanilla linen for the matting.
- **Her size.** A small outrigger has a hull of about 5 m and a float of about 3 m, 1.5 to 2 m out: hull, gap, float, three blocks across. Her mast is about 3 m, her spars 3 to 4 m, her booms 2.5 to 3 m, poles 8 to 10 cm thick. In the game her hull is the dugout's own, 4.5 m of the five logs' 5 m, and her mast, spars and booms take 3 m poles: measured off the model, 3.0 m, 2.98 m and 2.66 m.
- **Poles from a trunk.** A trunk a metre across, as the blocks are, is 0.785 m² of wood in section. It splits by halves along its grain, as rails are split: halved six times, it gives 64 billets of about 0.012 m², some 11 cm square, each dressed to the 8 to 10 cm round of a boom or spar. A row of two to five logs gives poles as long as it, at any length 64; she takes six.
- **Lashing by its length.** Each boom is lashed to both gunwales and to the float, thirteen turns a lashing (ten round, three frapping), each turn the girth of the boom and what it crosses: 4.2 m at a gunwale, 10 m at the float, 56 m in all, six of vanilla's ten-metre ropes. Her stays and sheet, each under ten metres, take one rope each.
- **Her sail by its area.** Her two spars spread a crab claw of 3.5 m², the upper one hoisted to the masthead. A linen block covers a square metre, and one cloth comes of one block: four cloths.
- **Every held action is real time over sixty,** as the canoe's. Setting a pole, about three minutes: three seconds. A rope's lashing or a stay, about a quarter of an hour: 15 seconds. Hewing a float's end to a point over 0.375 m takes off two thirds of that much log, about 18 litres; an axe stroke bites some 0.08 litres, a 10 cm edge 2 cm deep, and an axeman strikes 30 times a minute (H. Glaser, FAO): seven and a half minutes, seven and a half seconds. Lacing a cloth to the spars, about a quarter of an hour. Only the stroke rate is measured; the rest are estimates.
- **What she weighs.** Her hull is the dugout's 361 kg of oak; her float, 3 m of log 30 cm across, 143 kg of oak; her six poles 70 kg. A float or poles of another wood weigh as that wood, and her float floats her by its own volume: in oak she is 574 kg, 594 with her washstrakes, and swamped she still holds up 309 kg. She rises to a crest as slowly as the dugout; her float keeps her from rolling.
- **Washstrakes for the sea.** Pacific canoes raised a dugout's side with a washstrake a side, some 15 to 20 cm (a Tikopia hull 43 cm deep carries a 15 cm one; Hawaiian mo'o of 6 to 8 inches), and an end piece lashed over each end (Tobi, Kapingamarangi; Haddon and Hornell, *Canoes of Oceania*), sewn through holes 3 inches apart over a split batten (the Mont-Dore canoe). Hers are sawn planks, 15 cm: eight along her sides and two end pieces, sewn with 118 stitches of 30 cm, 35 m, four ropes. They lift her side from 38 cm to 53 cm with her helmsman and add 422 litres of hold, so a 2 m swell that boards the dugout runs past her.

## Building a coracle and a currach

- **Laths on the ground.** A Teifi coracle was begun as a lattice of split laths laid flat on the ground, seven along and seven across, their ends bent up later into a gunwale plaited of three soaked hazel rods (Hornell, *British Coracles and Irish Curraghs*, 1938). A currach was built upside down round her gunwale laid out on the ground, her ribs bent over it and set into it (the Boyne and Aran currachs, Hornell). In the game, laths of any wood stand in for willow, hazel and oak, and vanilla rope for twine and thongs.
- **Their size, and their laths.** The coracle is a Teifi one, 1.5 m by 1.07 m, its laths rising 0.35 m to the gunwale ([Museum Wales](https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/3ed081da-c3ef-3db4-b58a-dcabaf25c2fe)): her lattice laths are 1.2 to 1.9 m along their bend, so 2 m laths, and her gunwale plaits 4.2 m, so 5 m ones. The currach's gunwale is one 5 m lath a side, its ends drawn together: round her sheer it comes to 4.95 m, which gives her 4.7 m of length at her 1.2 m beam. Her ribs run 1.5 to 2.1 m over the gunwale, so 3 m laths, and her stringers 4.4 m.
- **Rope by its length.** One rope ties a dozen of the coracle's 37 bottom crossings, a few turns of cord each, about 0.8 m: three ropes. Each currach end is bound with some ten metres: one rope an end. A cover is laced to the gunwale at short intervals (Hornell), every 5 cm, each turn round the gunwale and the hide's edge some 15 cm of cord: the coracle's 4 m gunwale takes 16 m, two ropes, the currach's 9.9 m 40 m, four.
- **A small hide is a section.** A cover is sewn of sections of equal area, one small hide's each. Vanilla tans a small, medium, large and huge hide into 1, 2, 3 and 5 leather, so every size fills whole sections; a large hide is a moose's, some 3.5 m², so a small one is about 1.2 m². By the models the coracle's cover is 2.1 m², two sections, and the currach's 8.6 m², eight. An ordinary ox hide covered a frame about 6 by 4 feet (Hornell), one coracle.
- **What the hide is.** Any hide before leather goes on, and each keeps afloat as it really does. Green hide keeps a day at 20 °C, two weeks at 5 °C and three weeks at 0 °C before it putrefies ([CSIRO](https://meatupdate.csiro.au/data/MEAT_TECHNOLOGY_UPDATE_98-3.pdf)), 5.8 times longer for every 10 °C colder; once past its keeping it rots through in as long again, and the water it lies in sets the pace. Umiak skins, untanned, were taken out and dried every day or they rotted: dried ashore, a hide keeps afresh. Soaked and scraped hide is as raw; salted hide keeps half a day more, as tanneries soak its salt out in 8 to 20 hours; bark-tanned prepared hide keeps a year afloat, as the *Brendan*'s oak-tanned ox hides crossed the Atlantic and umiak covers lasted two to four years. A wet cover weighs with the water it takes up: a fresh hide some 14% of its weight, a salted one 45%, leather some 30%.
- **Seams.** Where one hide meets the next, a seam weeps once the grease has washed out of it: every hide after the first adds one.
- **Every held action is real time over sixty,** as the canoe's. A rib or a stringer, three seconds; a coracle's gunwale plait, some ten minutes, ten seconds; stretching and sewing a small hide's worth of cover, some twenty minutes, twenty seconds, and a bigger hide as much longer; a rope, 15 seconds. Turning a currach over is one heave.
- **What they weigh.** The coracle's 2.1 m² of hide 5 mm thick at 860 kg/m³ is some 9 kg, her 35 m of laths some 10 kg of willow: about 20 kg, where Hornell gives one-man coracles at 18 to 40 lb, a hide cover nearly double a cloth one's. The currach's 8.6 m² of hide is some 37 kg, her 83 m of laths some 30 kg of ash, with a thwart pole: about 70 kg. A frame of another wood weighs and holds as that wood.

## Where we bent reality, and what is not yet real

- **A stove-in crack is 25 cm²**, a hole 5.6 cm across, set for play rather than measured; a blow must break the shell around it.
- **A point of weapon damage is 200 J**, not yet measured: an iron axe's swing comes to 600 J, more than the 67 to 312 J measured for real strikes.
- **What backs a hide or binds a reed boat** is read off the models: how much of a side the laths cover, how far apart the lashings lie. The bark canoe's bark is 5 mm, for play.
- **How far reed soaks** (up to five times its weight), how fast it dries (four times its weight a sunny day) and how it rots (a third of what each drying drives out) is history's pace, not a measurement: a reed boat past saving after a few soakings, where the *balsas*, dried after every trip, last months. Only how fast it takes the water up is measured, from the caballito. That her ends sag in a straight line with her soak, rather than by the bending of wet reed, is ours too: nobody has measured it.
- **A whole bamboo culm weighs 230 kg/m³**, for play: its wall of 630 kg/m³ taken a tenth of its diameter thick, hollow between the nodes.
- **A hide cover's seams** seep once ungreased, 15 litres a game hour at each seam across a currach and 12 across a coracle's shorter one, and a greasing lasts two and a half days afloat: Aran crews dressed their currachs every few days, and the litres are ours.
- **Oiled hides and pelts keep three times as long as raw hide** afloat, an estimate: fat dressing sheds water but is no tannage, and brain- and fat-dressed hides wash out and harden when wet ([leather-dictionary.com](https://leather-dictionary.com/index.php/Brain_tanning)), but no keeping time was measured for them.
- **The waterline lies level.** A hull doesn't heel yet, so a side crack leaks by how low she sits, not by which side the wind leans her to; heel comes in [Seamanship](Roadmap#seamanship). How fast a hull settles after a crest, 0.3 of critical in heave, is ours too.
- **Cargo weighs only its containers.** A player carries loads no real crew could lift, so what is in a basket or chest adds nothing to a knock; the containers count at their own empty weight. Mail, which the game slows less than its real 10 to 14 kg would, comes out light.
- **The uncut wood backs a side by its depth.** What backs a side below the cut is the solid core across the beam, but the mod counts the depth left uncut, the same as under the bottom. Both are many times the 5 cm wall; the difference shows only on the last hollowings.
