What Rough Seas' hulls stand on: how they take a blow, float, sail, leak, ride the swell and are built, and where we bent it. What moves them through the water is on [The science of a body in the water](The-Science-of-a-Body-in-the-Water), and the waves that strike them on [The science of the waves](The-Science-of-the-Waves).

## Knocks

- **A knock is measured as a fall is.** The game hurts a falling body once, on the tick it lands, by how far it really fell. A hull is knocked the same way: once, on the step a rock first stops it, by how fast the hull really moved toward the rock over the tenth of a second before, from its change of position. Held against the rock, by a current or by its rider paddling on, it strikes the rock no more, however hard it is driven.
- **Water in the bilge sloshes.** Water with a free surface doesn't stop with the hull: it runs on, piles against the bow a moment later and spends part of its energy in the splash. A hull brimful moves almost as one body, so the mod counts the water's weight by how full the bilge is. The part left free to slosh then peaks at half full, which is the [free surface effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface_effect) naval architects guard against.
- **Who is aboard.** Each rider is the one reference body of 75 kg that swims ([A body in the water](The-Science-of-a-Body-in-the-Water#a-body-in-the-water)), with what they carry: their armour, and the tools and weapons in their hotbar by their type, at real analogues' masses, listed in `config/person.json`. The backpack and materials weigh nothing, the game's own convention.
- **Armour's weight comes from its stride.** The game gives armour no weight, only a slower walk. The mod reads the weight back from that penalty, calibrated on plate: tested suits of field armour weighed 35 ± 5 kg (Askew, Formenti and Minetti 2012, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 279), and the game's full plate slows its wearer by 0.42, 0.14 a piece, so armour weighs 83 kg to the unit of slowdown. The game's full chain then weighs 7.5 kg, against a mail hauberk of about 9.5 kg. Another mod's armour weighs by its own penalty.
- **Rams.** Two hulls that meet, at the speed they really closed by, from both hulls' change of position, lose the energy of that closing speed held by their reduced mass, ½ μ v², μ = m₁m₂ / (m₁ + m₂), as in any inelastic collision; each hull takes half.

## Hulls

- **A knock, a ram, rapids or lightning** loads the whole frame of a planked or carved hull: its energy is weighed against the hull's capacity, and cracks its place by that share. A hull's capacity scales by its wood's modulus of rupture against oak's, from the [Wood Database](https://www.wood-database.com/): ebony 158 MPa, purpleheart 152, bamboo 130, birch 114, maple 109, walnut 101, oak 97, larch 90, acacia 87, pine 83, bald cypress 73, redwood 62, aged wood 49, kapok 36. A species with no published value counts as 93.5, the middle of the vanilla woods.
- **A framed hull takes a knock at its skin and the frame behind.** The coracle, the bark canoe and the currach are skins over laths, and a blow against them meets the skin where it lands and the member behind it, not the whole boat. Hide stretches: wet cowhide, pickled, holds 26.6 MPa and stretches 60% before it parts (*Tensile Properties of Indigenous Kenyan Boran Pickled and Tanned Bovine Hide*), taken here as stretching evenly to it, 44 MPa. Pressed onto a lath, it passes the blow on and the lath breaks. Between two laths it sags as a membrane, its pull growing as the cube of its sag, 8 E t δ³ / g², g the gap between them, until that pull breaks both laths at their middles, each a beam 2 σ b h² / 3 L between the members it crosses, at soaked strength; the hide takes ¼ of that pull times its sag, about 21 J between a currach's stringers and 9 J between a coracle's willow laths, and both laths then break. A blow lands on a lath by the laths' share of the hull, their breadth over their spacing. Birch bark is stiff and splits along its grain, as a thick veneer (656 J/m², above), so it opens with the sheathing where that lines it, around the hole a blow stoves in, and the rib behind takes what is left; by the model the sheathing lines 48% of the canoe's sides and 98% of its bottom. A lath or a rib, 1 by 6 cm, breaks by its impact bending work across its section, the very quantity Kollmann's test measures: 41 J in ash, 45 in oak, 24 in willow and white cedar. Bark between two ribs opens and both ribs break behind it; bark is too stiff to sag, so it takes nothing before it splits. Its breaking is the split you see, the lath snapped under the skin; a knock short of it, or of opening the bark, leaves the place as it was, the hide and the wood springing back; twice that stoves the place in, and only what a stove-in place leaves goes on into the whole frame, which breaks the hull up past its capacity. The sources agree on which part gives: Adney and Chapelle (*The Bark Canoes and Skin Boats of North America*, 1964) quote LaHontan that bark canoes were easily damaged and had their bark mended at the end of each day, and the Malecite fitted canoe shoes to keep rocks and snags off the bark; Hornell (*British Coracles and Irish Curraghs*, 1938) has curragh builders cover the frame's rough ends, which would otherwise cut the hide, and coracles shooting rapids on rock-strewn rivers, their holes and rents patched with tarred cloth. A blow meets at most the two members around where it lands, as the reed boat's meets one bundle: no model of how the frame beyond them, and the hull as a whole, flexes under it.
- **A reed boat's bundles take a knock by crushing.** A bundle of stems is a cellular solid, as a foam or a crumple zone is: under a blow its stems give way at a steady stress and crush until they are packed solid, and the force on what binds them never rises past that stress meanwhile (Gibson and Ashby, *Cellular Solids*, 1997). A reed stalk pressed across gives way at 1.02 MPa, short of its failure at 25% (Li and Cui 2025, *Latin American Journal of Solids and Structures* 22(4)), and a cellular solid packs solid at 1 − 1.4 times its density over its cell walls': bundled reed at 270 kg/m³ over plant cell wall at 1500, three quarters of its depth. A knock crushes the bundle along the metre of it a block's face meets, 19 cm across as the boat's model draws it: 1.02 MPa × 0.75 × 0.030 m³, some 23 kJ, which only a ram at 18 m/s with its rider aboard would reach. What a blow leaves after that strains its lashings, which part once it has stretched them to breaking: its 80 m of 1 cm cord, as flax rope 7.6 kN at that size, stretched a tenth of its length as natural fibre ropes break at, 10 to 12% (manila and sisal, Machovec rope comparison), take ½ × 7.6 kN × 0.1 × 80 m, about 31 kJ, before they have all parted; the 80 m are the cord its model counts. So a knock, a ram, rapids or a bolt of lightning leaves the boat whole; a blast past 23 kJ tears it by what is left, and one past 53 kJ breaks it up. Nothing was measured of a reed boat hitting rock; at sea, Heyerdahl's papyrus *Ra* broke its steering oars ([Kon-Tiki Museum](https://kon-tiki.no/en/heyerdahls-expeditions/ra-1969-ra-ii-1970)) and *Abora III* its leeboards, and neither lost its bundles.
- **A blow on the shell** breaks it around a hole: its toughness times the hole's rim times the shell's thickness. Woods' toughness is their impact bending work at 12% moisture (Kollmann et al., in the Norwegian handbook *Wood – nature's most beautiful raw materials*, 1999): birch 94 kJ/m², beech 83, oak 75, ash 68, maple 65, elm 60, linden 50, pine and spruce 40; a wood not in the table counts as 60, their median. Gaboon ebony, brittle for its weight, gives 26 by ISO 13061-10, and Indian rosewood 46 (Liu et al., *J. Wood Sci.* 66:14, 2020). A wood with no measurement takes its nearest measured kin's: willow aspen's, larch pine's, walnut birch's, koa and purpleheart Indian rosewood's, and thuja, bald cypress, redwood and kapok spruce's and pine's. Aged wood, with no real species, takes half of oak's, as it does for its strength. In oak, a sailboat's 19 mm plank gives to about 250 J, a dugout's 5 cm wall to about 670 J, and the bark canoe's bark, where its sheathing of oak laths lines it, to about 43 J. A weapon strikes with 200 J a point of its damage, a blast or a bolt with 1000 J a point.
- **A cut** spends its work on parting the fibres alone: softwood's work of fracture across the grain is 9.2 kJ/m² (*Nature* 252, 116, 1974) against 40 kJ/m² of impact bending work, 0.23 of it. Hide tears at 17 kJ/m² (pig skin by trouser tear, Comley and Fleck 2010); a lashing, unmeasured as rope, as flax in epoxy, 5.7 kJ/m². Bark splits along its grain as a thick birch veneer, at birch's work of fracture, 656 J/m² (*Wood Science and Technology*, 2024).
- **A crack as you see it** runs as far as the shell bends between what holds it, its span: 54 cm up a dugout's carved side, 37 cm across a coracle's laths, 25 cm between a bark canoe's ribs, 22 cm between a currach's stringers, each measured on its model. Wood and bark crack along the grain. A place stove in leaks through 25 cm², a hole 5.6 cm across, but the shell broken around it is the span's length, pushed inward. A real crack opens a few millimetres; it is drawn a voxel's fraction wide, at least 9 cm long, so that it can be seen, as laths are drawn thicker than they weigh.
- **A patch takes its real time over sixty**, as building does ([Building a bark canoe](#building-a-bark-canoe)). Bark sewn over a hole with root and its edges pitched, as canoes had their bark mended at the end of each day (Adney and Chapelle), about a quarter of an hour: 15 seconds. A patch of hide sewn over a hole or rent (Hornell), as long as sewing one section of a cover, some twenty minutes: 20 seconds; rubbing rendered fat into a hide some ten minutes: 10 seconds. A graving piece cut from a log, fitted into the cracked wood and pegged, about half an hour: 30 seconds. A length of plank let in over a split and fastened to the frames, about twenty minutes: 20 seconds. Flax twine bound round a lashing, about a quarter of an hour: 15 seconds. All of them are estimates.
- **No two pieces of wood are alike.** Clear wood scatters around its table value with a coefficient of variation of 16% in bending strength, 25% in impact bending and 34% in toughness (Wood Handbook, FPL-GTR-282, table 5-6), lognormally; every blow meets its own piece.

## Breakers

- **A breaker's pressure.** A spilling roller presses as a stream does, ½ρc². A plunging crest slams a flat panel at 1.2 times its speed with a coefficient of 2π: ½ρ·2π·(1.2c)² (DNV-RP-C205, 2010, §8.7.2.5 and §8.8.1.2). In deep water a spilling breaker presses about 6 kPa at 1 m high, 23 kPa at 4 m and 56 kPa at 10 m, a plunging one nine times that. Which breakers spill and which plunge: [Waves](The-Science-of-the-Waves#waves).
- **It bends the planking.** A strip under an even pressure breaks at its modulus of rupture times thickness² over 6 k span², k being 1/12 for a plank fastened at both ends, 1/8 for a lath propped at its ends and 1/2 for a dugout's side standing free from the bottom. The sailboat's planks are 19 mm on frames 24 cm apart, as Herreshoff's rules (1927) give a yacht of its size. Wet wood bends to 0.57 of its dry strength, the mean over eleven woods (Wood Handbook). In oak, the coracle's laths give at 21 kPa, the currach's at 61, the bark canoe at 61, the sides of the dugout, and of the outrigger built on one, at 160, and the sailboat's planks at 705; the raft's logs, bending over the metre its model draws between two lashings, at about 7 MPa.

## Floating

- **Archimedes.** A hull sinks until it pushes aside its own weight of seawater, 1025 kg/m³, so its draft is its full mass over 1025 times its waterplane, the area it cuts at the water. Each vessel's numbers are measured off its model, vanilla's raft off its logs and the sailboat off its planking (each builder in `bin/lib/hulls` prints them, and `bin/model build` writes them into the vessel's JSON): its weight from the wood, bark or hide it is drawn with at its real thickness (the sailboat keeps vanilla's 1900 kg), its depth from keel to gunwale, its hold the hollow inside it to the gunwale, and the deck that catches the rain the opening at its gunwale. So the sailboat holds 8209 litres under 15.1 m² of open deck. Its waterplane is set so that its hold full, with one aboard, brings its gunwale to the water. A raft and a reed boat are solid, pressed down through their own thickness. Each is drawn as deep in the water as it floats with one aboard.
- **What each material weighs.** Woods by their average dried weight in the [Wood Database](https://www.wood-database.com/): oak 675 kg/m³, pine 550, ebony 955, ekki 1065; kapok by CIRAD's Tropix, red mangrove by Galvão et al. (2025). Hide counts as dry leather, 860 kg/m³, and cast-iron ballast 7200 ([Engineering ToolBox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/density-solids-d_1265.html)); birch bark 560 (*Silva Fennica*, article 5113). A wood of no known species weighs 610, the middle of the vanilla woods. A hull's weight is given for its reference build, oak for a wooden one, and the same shape in another wood weighs by its density.
- **Ballast.** A keelboat carries about two fifths of its weight as ballast to stand up to its sail, so the sailboat does, in iron, and an oak one flooded goes down.
- **A body floats awash.** A human body is about as dense as seawater, so the water holds up the people aboard a flooded hull; their tools, their armour and the containers weigh on it.
- **Speed at vanilla's pace.** A hull that neither a rower of ours nor a sail of ours drives, vanilla's sailboat moved with its sail furled, still goes at vanilla's pace, slowed by its load: at the same power, drag grows with the wetted area, which grows as displacement to the ⅔, and with speed squared, so speed goes as displacement to the −2/9 of its full mass against its reference build with one 75 kg rider. Paddled, poled or rowed, its speed comes from its drag itself ([Driving it](The-Science-of-a-Body-in-the-Water#driving-it)); under sail, from the wind against that drag ([Under sail](#under-sail)).

## Under sail

The wind pushes a vessel as the water does: one more force in the sum that moves its hull ([How the waves move a hull](The-Science-of-a-Body-in-the-Water#how-the-waves-move-a-hull)), on the same hull whether anyone steers it or not. Only the [outrigger](Outrigger-Canoe) and the [sailboat](Sailboat) take the wind so far.

- **The wind grows with height.** Our wind is the Beaufort scale's, the wind 10 m above the sea (WMO). Over open sea it grows with height z as (z / 10 m)^0.11 (Hsu, Meindl and Gilhousen 1994), so each surface meets the wind at its own height above the water, never lower than 10 cm.
- **The wind a sail feels is apparent:** the wind at its height less the vessel's own velocity over the ground. A canoe running at 3 m/s before a 9 m/s wind feels 6 m/s; reaching across it, the apparent wind swings ahead and grows.
- **Windage.** What stands above the water, hull and furled rig, is a bluff body: drag ½ ρ C_d A u |u| across its side area and along its front area, at their centre, with air at 1.225 kg/m³ (the International Standard Atmosphere at 15 °C) and C_d 1.28, a flat plate across the wind (NASA Glenn). It only pushes downwind.
- **A set sail is a wing.** Its force is ½ ρ V_a² S times a drive coefficient along the hull and a side coefficient across it, S its area and V_a the apparent wind, both coefficients read at the angle the apparent wind comes from, the sail trimmed to that angle. The windage of the hull and rig pushes on beside it.
  - **The crab claw** takes Marchaj's wind-tunnel tests of a Polynesian crab claw, heeled 10° at 18 knots (AYRS 111, *Rig Efficiency*, Fig. 23, checked against the total force of Fig. 6): drive 1.46 and side 0.87 with the wind abeam, side force highest at 50° (1.48), drive highest at 120° to 130° (1.58), the force leaning to windward past 120°. Below 20° it luffs and lifts nothing, and the windage pushes again. Marchaj found it "only average closehauled, but outstanding when the apparent wind is between 50° and 150°".
  - **The square sail** takes Nomoto's tunnel tests of a single square sail, as Palmer redraws them (*Windward Sailing Capabilities of Ancient Vessels*, IJNA 38(2), 2009, Fig. 10): lift C_L and drag C_D over its trims, from 0.69 and 0.25 to 0 and 1.14; its first trims match the same sail's tunnel data of Masuyama, Nomoto et al. (MARSIM 2003, Fig. 13). Cloth carries pressure only normal to itself, plus friction along the wind, so each pair stands at the incidence α = atan((C_D − 0.15) / C_L), 8° to 90°, where 0.15 is the friction drag Masuyama et al. add to the pressure forces to meet the tunnel's. Edge-on to the wind the sail keeps its friction alone. At each heading β the yard is braced to the turn its stays allow that drives most, C_L sin β − C_D cos β, with C_L cos β + C_D sin β across, both read at the incidence that turn gives. So close-hauled, with the yard braced as far round as it goes, the sail draws only what that incidence gives.
  - **Taken aback**, the wind on its wrong face, a sail is a flat plate: ½ ρ C_d S (n·V_a) |n·V_a| along its normal n. It drives nothing. The crab claw is aback when the wind comes over its lee side; the square sail when the wind strikes its fore face at every turn its stays allow, as head to wind: Masuyama et al.'s Fig. 15 shows the same sail caught aback within 15° of the wind.
- **The sail's shape** is one function of where it is held, how much is set, its camber and how full the wind blows it, which the physics reads for its centre of effort and the game draws, every point of the cloth, in the vessel's own frame. Its depth is 12% of its chord when full, as Marchaj's tunnel sails were cut, a parabola across it. It fills as the wind's pressure p outweighs its cloth: depth p / (p + w g), the cloth w 0.61 kg/m², as experimental flax sails weigh 500 to 720 g/m² (Propylaeum, Heidelberg). With the wind abeam, the outrigger's sail stands two fifths full at 2 m/s of apparent wind and nearly full at 6 m/s.
- **The crab claw trims itself.** The yard turns on its halyard about the line from the tack to the masthead, and the boom about the yard on its lashing. The sail settles where the moments of its spars' weight, the wind's force at its centre and its sheet's pull balance about that line: the sheet pulls `−M·a / (l·a)`, M the moment of the rest about the tack, l the sheet's lever and a the axis. It takes only turns where no spar or cloth meets the mast, its shore or a stay.
- **The rig holds it.** The force reaches the hull through the fittings that hold the sail. On the crab claw: the yard's heel in its step, the halyard at the masthead, the sheet at its lead; the masthead is held by the mast, the shroud and a running stay, the backstay where it can only pull and else the forestay, three lines solved for the three unknown pulls. On the square sail the yard turns about the mast, its lee sheet holds it, and the mast takes the rest at its step. Each line's tension is known, and what they hand the hull sums to the sail's force and the weight of its spars and cloth, at the points where each really acts.
- **The square sail braces about its mast.** Its yard turns about the mast as far as its stays let it, to the turn that drives most at the incidence it gives.
- **Leeway and turning.** The side force is met by the hull's own cross-flow drag, the same drag a current pulls with ([Carried by the current](The-Science-of-a-Body-in-the-Water#carried-by-the-current)): the hull slides sideways until the water pushes back as hard, the outrigger by about 7° at 3 m/s under 100 N of side force. The force's moment about the centre of the hull's side under the water turns it, against the water's drag on its turning, unless a line holds it by the bow.
- **What it comes to.** With a 9 m/s breeze abeam, the outrigger makes about a third of the wind's speed. Run before the wind, the crab claw stands across the canoe; head to wind, the windage pushes it astern.

## Leaks

- **Torricelli's law.** Water runs in through a hole at √(2 g h), h the depth of the hole under the waterline. Through a sharp-edged hole the jet narrows, and the flow is 0.61 of the hole's area times that speed: a contraction of 0.62 by a velocity coefficient of 0.98 (US Bureau of Reclamation, *Water Measurement Manual*, chapter 2).
- **Against a real table.** A hull stove in at one place leaks like a 5.6 cm hole: three litres a second 20 cm down. A hard knock's crack, a quarter of that, lets in 56 litres a minute a foot down, a little over what [Landfall Navigation](https://landfallnavigation.com/)'s flooding table gives a 2.5 cm hole there: 45 litres a minute. A crack under the water already inside the hull leaks by the difference between the sea outside and the water in it, so a bottom crack lets in as much while it fills as when it was dry.
- **Where a side cracks.** Rocks and quays meet a side at the turn of the bilge, a quarter of the way up from the keel: above the water in a light boat, below it once the boat is laden.
- **Reed.** Bound into bundles, dry totora weighs 270 kg/m³: a Huanchaco *caballito de totora* 2.44 × 0.41 × 0.23 m weighs 41 kg dry (Smithsonian Folklife Festival), and the reed boat, 3 × 0.36 × 0.19 m of the same build, 43 kg in its bundles' 160 litres. A game reed weighs about 0.15 kg, from the rush mat: one square metre of it from 8 reeds, as a real one weighs 1 to 1.5 kg; so its bundles take 288 reeds, and a rope's six reeds make a cord of 90 g a metre, as a real 1 cm one is. A bundle of reed soaks: the caballito weighs some 27 kg more after a day in the sea, so reed takes up two thirds of its weight in water a day afloat; on Lake Titicaca the totora *balsas* are dried ashore between trips and last months, and Heyerdahl's papyrus *Ra II*, with nowhere to dry, settled about 10 cm a day crossing the Atlantic.
- **A reed boat's ends sag as it soaks.** They stand up only by the stiffness of their bound reed against their own weight; soaked, the reed is heavier and limp. Nothing holds them up, as nothing held up the stern of Heyerdahl's first *Ra*. How far they sag goes straight with how much of the water the boat can take before it sinks the reed has taken, drawn at four steps: dry, they stand; about to go down with nothing aboard, they lie flat.

## Riding the swell

- **Heave and pitch.** A hull bobs on a natural period of 2π √((M + A) / (ρ g A_w)), its mass M and the water A it drags with it. A flat waterplane drags the water of Lamb's elliptic disc, half of it at the free surface: (2/3) π ρ a b² / E(e) for half-length a and half-beam b (Lamb, *Hydrodynamics*, §373; Newman, *Marine Hydrodynamics*), strip theory's ½ ρ π (b/2)² a metre for a long hull. That is the high-frequency limit, the most a hull can carry. A wide, shallow hull drags several times its own mass: with its paddler, the bark canoe 4.5 times, the currach 8, the dugout 2.6. So it bobs slowly, at 1.1 s for the canoe, 1.3 s for the dugout and the currach. A change of load moves the hull on the same period: weight put aboard is a force on its mass and on the water it drags with it, so it sinks to its new waterline in a bob or two and is never thrown up past where it floated.
- **Heave damping.** A hull heaving radiates waves: per metre of it, b = ρ g² Ā² / ω³, with the radiated wave Ā ≈ 2 sin(kB/2) e^(−kT) of its beam B and draft T (Newman; Journée and Massie, *Offshore Hydromechanics*). At its heave period that is 0.2 to 0.4 of critical damping; no decay test of a small open boat was found, so ours damps at 0.3. A load put aboard all at once sinks it about a third past its new waterline before it settles.
- **Roll.** The US Coast Guard's rule for small fishing vessels, 46 CFR 28.575, gives the roll period as 2 C B / √GM, with C = 0.373 + 0.023 B/d − 0.00043 L from its beam B, draft d and length L; GM is that of a box hull with its centre of gravity at half its depth. A small hull without bilge keels rolls on for several swings, at a damping of 0.06 to 0.1 of critical; ours rolls at 0.1.
- So a light canoe follows a long swell as the water lies, while a heavy, deep hull lets a short chop pass under it and rolls on its own longer period.

## Building a dugout

- **Fire, then the adze.** Where people had no metal, they hollowed a log by fire: Thomas Harriot saw the Algonquians of Roanoke make their canoes "only with the helpe of fire, harchets of stones, and shels" (*A Briefe and True Report*, 1588), and John White drew them at it, a fire burning along the log while others scrape. Burnt wood comes away far more easily than sound wood (the Butser Prometheus project, [EXARC](https://exarc.net/ark:/88735/10176)), so each fire is followed by scraping out its char down to sound wood.
- **How fast it burns.** A fire of three to five hours chars 6 to 13 mm into the log, and wet clay packed along the upper edges keeps it from burning through the sides ([Agency House](https://www.agencyhouse.org/dugout-canoe), 2021–2023). Builders lay the clay again each morning before the day's fire (the [PHMC dugout project](https://pahistoricpreservation.com/experimental-archaeology-the-historical-and-museum-commission-building-dugout-canoe/), 2005), and Wampanoag builders, keeping the fire going round the clock, burn out a mishoon in 6 to 10 days ([Plimoth Patuxet](https://plimoth.org/blog/the-making-of-a-mishoon)). The mod chars 2.5 mm an hour, so each of the six fires that hollow out the dugout's 0.51 m burns about 34 hours, a day and a half, about nine days in all.
- **The trunk the logs make.** Five logs lying end to end are a trunk 5 m long and a metre square, as the blocks are: the blank is that trunk where it lay, 5000 litres, three and a third tonnes of oak. Builders took the bark off first, "with certayne shells" (Harriot's caption to White's engraving, 1590; the Agency House log was peeled before its first burn), laid the fire on the bare top "accordinge to the lengthe of the bodye of the tree, sauinge at both the endes", and shaped the outside as they burnt it out (a Haida dugout begun as a squared log; the Wampanoag mishoon, flat on top under its fire). Each of the six hewings takes a sixth of the 3114 litres outside the finished hull with the char: the top down to a level sheer 0.56 m above the keel, the bottom round and raked up under each end, until the hull is the trunk's whole 5 m long and 0.9 m wide, its ends solid where the fire was kept off them and the hollow ending in a rounded scoop, its walls 5 cm as the Dufuna canoe's (de Bry's engraving, the Timucuan and Carpow logboats).
- **The bark, a stroke a side.** Oak bark is about 2 cm thick, and the trunk's 15 sides of a log, top and both flanks of each, come off a stroke each. Peeling a log really takes hours; like the hewing it is left at a stroke every 2 seconds, half a minute's hold.
- **Clay by the game's own arithmetic.** In vanilla's clay forming a ball of clay lays 25 voxels. Wet clay one voxel thick along both edges and over the solid ends covers 461 voxels on the fresh trunk and 252 on the last fire's, as the hull narrows: 19 balls to 11. The dried clay stays on the rim until the hewing takes the rim down, and the next fire takes fresh clay, as builders packed it again each morning.
- **A fire of coals, not flames.** "Heat rises, so the canoe rarely flames visibly," and most of the charring came once the fire had burned down to a bed of coals (Agency House). So the fire is drawn catching in its first hour, then burning down to embers over the rest of its day and a half, with little flame and much smoke, as vanilla's pit kiln smoulders.
- **An unguarded rim burns through.** With no clay on it, the rim chars at the same 2.5 mm an hour as the hollow. The dugout's walls are 5 cm, so 20 hours of a fire without clay char right through them; a full fire is 34, and the blank is spoiled.
- **Char crumbles.** Burnt wood has lost its fibres, so an axe doesn't have to cut it: a blow breaks it away down to sound wood wherever the edge sweeps. One stroke across the 0.8 m hollow, under a 10 cm edge, takes out a strip of the day's 8.5 cm of char, about 7 litres, and a hewing's 239 litres take 36 strokes. A woodsman's best rhythm with an axe is 25 to 35 strokes a minute (H. Glaser, physiology of forestry work, [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/X5373E/x5373e04.htm)), a stroke every 2 seconds. About a minute a hewing. The sound wood the hull loses outside at each hewing, 519 litres, is shaped with the same strokes: really nearly four hours of adze work, it is left untimed so a hewing stays a minute's hold. The minute is shared out as the work goes, the char cut from the hollow a third at a time, then the outside a third at a time, twelve seconds each.
- **Rain puts it out.** Rain douses the fire as it douses any of the game's fires, and no fire burns in a hollow with water in it.
- **The blank weighs.** Until it is cut out and hewn away, the wood is there: a fresh oak blank is the square trunk, 3375 kg, and floats 0.66 m deep, against the finished dugout's 304 kg, 450 litres of wood around a 1436-litre hold. The hull's depth and waterplane go from the trunk's to the hull's a sixth a hewing. A blank of a wood heavier than water sinks.
- **The uncut wood backs the hull.** Below the cut, a blow on its side or bottom meets the solid wood behind the 5 cm wall as well as the wall, and resists by the thickness of both: a copper axe that would stave in a finished side only cracks a fresh blank.
## Building a bark canoe

- **One sheet, no saw.** The peoples of the northern woodlands built their canoes with stone and bone tools, no saw: bark peeled from a standing birch, a frame split from cedar with wedges, lashings of spruce root and seams of spruce gum (Adney and Chapelle, *The Bark Canoes and Skin Boats of North America*, 1964). In the game the bark comes off in one sheet, which is the canoe from the start; vanilla rope stands in for the root and vanilla's pitch glue, resin cooked with charcoal, for the gum.
- **Every held action is real time over sixty.** A game day passes in 48 real minutes, 30 times faster than the real one, and boat work is halved again, so that an hour of real work is a minute held. Each action is one piece of work. Peeling a five-metre sheet takes some 20 to 40 minutes: half a minute. Wedges are the exception: they go in blow by blow, so each blow the axe is seen to strike drives one a step deeper, at the pace wedges are driven with a maul, 30 to 40 blows a minute, a second and a half a blow. A steamed rib has to be driven home within a couple of minutes, before it cools ([Robin Wood](https://www.robin-wood.co.uk/wood-craft-blog/2014/09/28/birch-bark-canoe-build-final-chapter/), 2014): three seconds a lath. Ten metres of lashing, some fifty turns through holes made with an awl, take about a quarter of an hour, which would be 15 seconds a rope; the canoe's gunwales and stems take 21 ropes, so for play a rope is held as long as a sheathing lath, 3 seconds. Only the rib is measured; the rest are estimates.
- **Laths split from poles.** The model draws 29 laths: an inwale and an outwale on each side and three thwarts, a stem at each end, three strakes of sheathing on each side and fourteen ribs. Each names the length it takes, measured off the model along its run: the canoe's gunwales are 4.4 m, so 5 m laths, and its sheathing, running from one stem's heel to the other's, 3.3 m, so 4 m ones; its ribs 0.9 to 1.1 m, its thwarts 0.6 m and its stems 0.9 m, so any lath of 2 m or more. A piece takes any lath at least as long, cut down, the offcut lost. A pole, some 11 cm square, halved four times along its grain gives 16 laths of about 1 by 6 cm, a canoe rib's size (⅜ by 2½ to 3 inches, Adney and Chapelle): two poles frame a canoe.
- **Lashing by its length.** The Malecite lashed bark to gunwale in groups of eight to ten turns of root, about two inches apart (Adney and Chapelle). Around two gunwales of 1¼ inch and the bark between them, a turn is 20 cm of root, so each metre of gunwale takes 23 m, and the canoe 209 m with its stems: 21 ropes, at the 10 m a vanilla rope pays out.
- **Pitch by the pot.** A bark canoe takes about a quart of pitch (WCHA forum, [bark pitch questions](https://forums.wcha.org/threads/bark-pitch-questions.6357/latest)): a litre, the pot of glue vanilla cooks from two resin and two charcoal powder, a quarter litre on each of its four seams.
- **Wet laths, set ribs.** Ribs were soaked or scalded, bent in, and left to set; bent ribs set in a couple of days (the [Lac du Flambeau build](https://canoe.csumc.wisc.edu/LdFCanoe_subpage_Construction_chronological.html)). Water enters a thin lath by the same diffusion it leaves by, so a lath soaks through in a barrel in as long as it takes to dry: two days each way, at 0.08 litres a metre of lath, about a third of its dry weight, from air-dry past the fibre saturation point. The canoe is finished when its last rib has set.
- **Soaked under stones.** Before barrels, timber was soaked in a river or a pond, weighted down so it stayed under. A lath floats even soaked: white cedar of 400 kg/m³ takes up a third of its weight and stays near 530, so 32 laths of 5 m, 1 by 6 cm, lift about 45 kg under water. The game gives a stone no mass, so the largest bundle sets it: a full stack of 64 stones holds those laths down, 0.7 kg each under water, a 7 cm stone of granite. Shorter laths lift less, in proportion to their length, and take stones in the same proportion: 26 for 2 m, 38 for 3 m, 51 for 4 m. The bundle soaks its laths in the two days a barrel takes, since water enters them the same way.
- **The canoe's sides go up as the Malecite raised them.** The gunwale frame, inwales joined by thwarts, was laid on the bark flat on the building bed and weighted with stones; the bark was turned up around it and clamped upright between stakes; then the frame was lifted on posts to the sheer and the bark sewn to it (Adney and Chapelle). The bark outside the frame was first slashed from its edge in to the frame, near each thwart's end and between them, so that it turned up without buckling where the hull narrows; the slashes later became the gores, sewn and gummed. Once the frame stood at the sheer, the ends were shaped round their stem pieces and the surplus cut away. In the game the inwales and thwarts lie flat on the sheet as they are laid, the middle thwart first. The last thwart turns its middle up square around the frame, where its sides run straight and need no slash. Each of four knife slashes, at its four gores, the inner pair first, lets the next length turn up, out to the sheet's end, so the whole five-metre sheet stands as tall as its width leaves and its ends stand tallest, as the builders' longest stakes stood at the ends. The first outwale raises the frame to the canoe's sheer and the bark is sewn to it in its finished form, the sheet's surplus still standing past each end, until a knife trims each, the stern first, the offcut lost. The rib heads are forced in under the inwales, never through them. Lying flat, the canoe floats as a sheet 3.75 cm thick; turned up, 25 cm deep; raised, as deep as its hull.
- **What the canoe is as it grows.** By the model its bark is 4.8 m² at 5 mm, 13 kg, and its 56 m of laths 1 by 6 cm, 11.7 kg in white cedar: a 25 kg cedar build. Its laths add their share as they go in, the volume cedar would have at the weight of the wood split: a frame of oak makes it 36 kg, of birch 35. A knock is taken by as much of its build as is laid, in the strength of its laths' wood against cedar's: a bare sheet takes half the finished cedar canoe's 2400 J, a finished oak one 5200. Its sheathing backs the bark and bends against a breaker only where it is laid, and its hold holds water only once the seams are pitched.

## Building an outrigger

- **A dugout built onward.** Austronesian outriggers were built without metal or saw: a burnt and hewn dugout, a float of a smaller log shaped with an adze and pointed at its ends, two or three booms across the hull lashed with fibre cord to the gunwales and the float, and a crab-claw sail of matting on two spars, with a lashed mast and cord stays. The rig went onto a finished hull, and was sometimes moved from one hull to another. In the game the hull is the dugout's own, with its cracks and its bilge; vanilla rope stands in for the cord and vanilla linen for the matting.
- **Its size.** A small outrigger has a hull of about 5 m and a float of about 3 m, 1.5 to 2 m out: hull, gap, float, three blocks across. Its mast is about 3 m, its spars 3 to 4 m, its booms 2.5 to 3 m, poles 8 to 10 cm thick. In the game its hull is the dugout's own, the five logs' whole 5 m. Its mast is 3 m, 0.6 of the hull, as the Fijian mast was "a little longer than half the length of the canoe" (27 ft 6 in on 46 ft, Haddon and Hornell, *Canoes of Oceania*, 1:314); its yard and boom 1.24 and 1.18 times the mast, as in the Fijian thamakau's rigging plan (Hornell 1936, fig. 230): 3.72 m and 3.54 m, which take 4 m poles. Measured off the model, its booms are 2.66 m and its mast's shore 1.96 m.
- **The rig, as history built it.** Every single-outrigger canoe with this rig kept its float to windward and its sail to leeward, and changed tack by shunting: the sail's foot was carried to the other end, which became the bow (Haddon and Hornell on the Marshalls, the Marianas, Fiji and Samoa). So the outrigger's mast stands on the middle boom by the gunwale on the float's side, as in Fiji and the Marianas, raked 26° toward the sail's foot, and running stays to both ends rake it toward whichever end the foot is at, as at every shunt. A shroud and a wooden shore go out toward the float; the shore keeps the mast from falling onto the float when the sail is taken aback (Fiji's *rokoroko*, Anson's proa). The yard hangs from a halyard through the masthead, bent on two thirds of the way up it and a fifth of the mast long, its heel in a step at either end; the boom is lashed to the yard's heel, 25° open from it (Hornell 1936, figs. 229–234 and 300), and the sheet is bent on a quarter of the boom's length from its end, as in Fiji. The sail is reefed by rolling its foot round the boom, as in the Marianas, and furled with both spars let down along the lee side. Our hull stays the dugout's, symmetric, as Fiji's and Palau's were; the Marshallese hull, flat on its lee side, is not ours.
- **Poles from a trunk.** A trunk a metre across, as the blocks are, is 0.785 m² of wood in section. It splits by halves along its grain, as rails are split: halved six times, it gives 64 billets of about 0.012 m², some 11 cm square, each dressed to the 8 to 10 cm round of a boom or spar. A row of two to five logs gives poles as long as it, at any length 64; the outrigger takes seven, two of them 4 m.
- **The float, hewn from its logs.** The three logs lie where they lay and become the float as they are, three cubic metres of wood. An adze hewed a float down from its log; here each of six passes hews it all round, a sixth of the way from the square logs to a round log 30 cm across with its ends pointed and lifted, 196 litres. Chip by chip, about 470 litres a pass, an axe biting some 0.08 litres a stroke 30 times a minute would take three or four minutes of holding a pass: a pass takes a minute, as long as a dugout's hewing, a call for play as the dugout's was. Until it is hewn the float weighs as the wood left in it, two tonnes in oak at first, and stands at the water as wide as it is.
- **Booms laid straight onto the float.** A float met its booms either through short stakes driven into it (the "short uprights driven into the outrigger" of the southern Pacific canoes, J. L. Young, 1876) or directly, the booms curved down to it (Hawaii, the Marshall Islands). The outrigger's is the direct kind: each boom is a crooked pole that takes its curve as it is laid, rests on the hull's gunwales and on the float's top, settles with every pass as the float shrinks beneath it, and is lashed straight onto the finished float.
- **Lashing by its length.** Each boom is lashed to both gunwales and to the float, thirteen turns a lashing (ten round, three frapping), each turn the girth of the boom and what it crosses: 3.6 m at a gunwale, 10 m at the float, 52 m in all, six of vanilla's ten-metre ropes. Its two stays, its shroud, its halyard and its sheet, each under ten metres, take one rope each.
- **The float's lashings, as the raft's.** The float stands 2 m out, so whatever the outrigger meets on that side meets it first, and what gives is the cord that holds it, not the float's log. Nothing was measured for an outrigger's lashings, so they take what the raft's do: its three ropes give way to 1200 J, 40 J a metre of lashing, and the 52 m of rope its model counts to 2067 J. One and a half twines bind up a rope's worth, as on the raft, nine for its six ropes. Once the lashings give out, nothing holds the float or the booms, and the mast stands on stays made fast to them: it all comes away, and the outrigger is the dugout it was rigged on.
- **The sail by its area.** The yard and the boom spread a crab claw of 2.78 m². A linen block covers a square metre, and one cloth comes of one block: three cloths.
- **Every held action is real time over sixty,** as the canoe's. Setting a pole, about three minutes: three seconds. A rope's lashing or a stay, about a quarter of an hour: 15 seconds. Lacing a cloth to the spars, about a quarter of an hour. Only the stroke rate is measured; the rest are estimates.
- **What it weighs.** By the model, its hull is the dugout's 304 kg of oak; its float, 196 litres, 132 kg of oak; its seven poles, 9 cm booms and mast and 7.5 cm shore and spars, 136 litres, 92 kg. A float or poles of another wood weigh as that wood, and its float floats it by its own volume: in oak the outrigger is 528 kg, 563 with its washstrakes, and swamped it still holds up 289 kg. It rises to a crest as slowly as the dugout; its float keeps it from rolling.
- **Washstrakes for the sea.** Pacific canoes raised a dugout's side with a washstrake a side, some 15 to 20 cm (a Tikopia hull 43 cm deep carries a 15 cm one; Hawaiian mo'o of 6 to 8 inches), and an end piece lashed over each end (Tobi, Kapingamarangi; Haddon and Hornell, *Canoes of Oceania*), sewn through holes 3 inches apart over a split batten (the Mont-Dore canoe). The outrigger's are sawn planks, 15 cm: five along each side from one end of its log to the other and a board across each end, sewn with 146 stitches of 30 cm, 44 m, five ropes. They lift its side from 42 cm to 56 cm with its helmsman and add 574 litres of hold, its plan inside them over the hollow and the solid ends alike, so a 2 m swell that boards the dugout runs past it.

## Building a coracle and a currach

- **Laths on the ground.** A Teifi coracle was begun as a lattice of split laths laid flat on the ground, seven along and seven across, their ends bent up later into a gunwale plaited of three soaked hazel rods (Hornell, *British Coracles and Irish Curraghs*, 1938). A currach was built upside down round its gunwale laid out on the ground, its ribs bent over it and set into it (the Boyne and Aran currachs, Hornell). In the game, laths of any wood stand in for willow, hazel and oak, and vanilla rope for twine and thongs.
- **Their size, and their laths.** The coracle is a Teifi one, 1.5 m by 1.07 m, its laths rising 0.35 m to the gunwale ([Museum Wales](https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/3ed081da-c3ef-3db4-b58a-dcabaf25c2fe)): its lattice laths are 0.9 to 1.9 m along their bend, so 2 m laths, and its gunwale plaits 4.2 m, so 5 m ones. The currach's gunwale is one 5 m lath a side, its ends drawn together: round its sheer it comes to 4.95 m, which gives the currach 4.8 m of length at its 1.2 m beam. Its twenty-two ribs run 1.4 to 2.1 m over the gunwale, so 2 m laths for thirteen of them and 3 m for the nine longest, and its stringers 4.4 to 4.5 m.
- **Laid as a pile.** Laths go down on the ground as vanilla piles them. Two-metre laths pile as the coracle's lattice, seven along and seven across: the pile's first layer is drawn from the same whole laths as the coracle's flat lattice, so one layer bound becomes the coracle with nothing moved. Longer laths lie lengthwise, the first two along the two sides of their block, so the currach's two gunwale laths lie a little inside the 1.08 m its model draws between them inside its hide; the first rope spreads them to it, a few centimetres a side, as it draws their bow ends together.
- **Rope by its length.** One rope ties nine of the coracle's 27 bottom crossings, those where both laths lie flat, a few turns of cord each, about 0.8 m: three ropes. Each currach end is bound with some ten metres: one rope an end. A cover is laced to the gunwale at short intervals (Hornell), every 5 cm, each turn round the gunwale and the hide's edge some 15 cm of cord: the coracle's 4 m gunwale takes 16 m, two ropes, the currach's 9.9 m 40 m, four.
- **A small hide is a section.** A cover is sewn of sections of equal area, one small hide's each. Vanilla tans a small, medium, large and huge hide into 1, 2, 3 and 5 leather, so every size fills whole sections; a large hide is a moose's, some 3.5 m², so a small one is about 1.2 m². By the models the coracle's cover is 2.2 m², two sections, and the currach's 8.7 m², eight. An ordinary ox hide covered a frame about 6 by 4 feet (Hornell), one coracle.
- **What the hide is.** Any hide before leather goes on, and each keeps afloat as it really does. Green hide keeps a day at 20 °C, two weeks at 5 °C and three weeks at 0 °C before it putrefies ([CSIRO](https://meatupdate.csiro.au/data/MEAT_TECHNOLOGY_UPDATE_98-3.pdf)), 5.8 times longer for every 10 °C colder; once past its keeping it rots through in as long again. A cover's time afloat counts at the temperature of each day's water, as days at 20 °C. Umiak skins, untanned, were taken out and dried every day or they rotted: a day in the sun ashore dries a cover, and it keeps afresh. Soaked and scraped hide is as raw; salted hide keeps half a day more, as tanneries soak its salt out in 8 to 20 hours; bark-tanned prepared hide keeps a year afloat, as the *Brendan*'s oak-tanned ox hides crossed the Atlantic and umiak covers lasted two to four years. A wet cover weighs with the water it takes up: a fresh hide some 14% of its weight, a salted one 45%, leather some 30%.
- **Seams.** Where one hide meets the next, a seam weeps once the grease has washed out of it: every hide after the first adds one. A seam weeps 15 litres a game hour for each 2.3 m of it, as long as the boat's model draws it: 12.6 litres across the currach's girth, 13 across the coracle's bowl.
- **Every held action is real time over sixty,** as the canoe's. A rib or a stringer, three seconds; a coracle's gunwale plait, some ten minutes, ten seconds; stretching and sewing a small hide's worth of cover, some twenty minutes, twenty seconds, and a bigger hide as much longer; a rope, 15 seconds. Turning a currach over is one heave.
- **What they weigh.** By the models, the coracle's 2.2 m² of hide 5 mm thick at 860 kg/m³ is 9 kg, its 32 m of laths 8 kg of willow: 17 kg, where Hornell gives one-man coracles at 18 to 40 lb. The currach's 8.7 m² of hide is 37 kg, its 83 m of laths and a 9 cm thwart pole 40 kg of ash: 77 kg, and 88.5 kg with its two oars.
- **A hide cut to fit.** A hide bigger than what is left of a cover is cut to it, and the rest comes back as the smaller hides it still makes: a huge hide sewn where two sections are left gives back a large one. A frame of another wood weighs and holds as that wood.

## Where we bent reality, and what is not yet real

- **A stove-in crack is 25 cm²**, a hole 5.6 cm across, set for play rather than measured; a blow must break the shell around it.
- **A bark canoe's rope is held 3 seconds**, as long as a sheathing lath, not the quarter of an hour's 15 seconds its lashing would take: at that pace its 21 ropes were a long wait.
- **A point of weapon damage is 200 J**, not yet measured: an iron axe's swing comes to 600 J, more than the 67 to 312 J measured for real strikes.
- **What backs a hide, lines a bark canoe or binds a reed boat** is read off the models: how much of a side and of the bottom the laths, the sheathing or the lashings cover, sampled over the hull's skin, and how far apart the members lie. The bark canoe's bark is 5 mm, for play.
- **How far reed soaks** (up to five times its weight), how fast it dries (four times its weight a sunny day) and how it rots (a third of what each drying drives out) is history's pace, not a measurement: a reed boat past saving after a few soakings, where the *balsas*, dried after every trip, last months. Only how fast it takes the water up is measured, from the caballito. That a reed boat's ends sag in a straight line with its soak, rather than by the bending of wet reed, is ours too: nobody has measured it.
- **A whole bamboo culm weighs 230 kg/m³**, for play: its wall of 630 kg/m³ taken a tenth of its diameter thick, hollow between the nodes.
- **A hide cover's seams** seep once ungreased, 15 litres a game hour for each 2.3 m of seam, and a greasing lasts two and a half days afloat: Aran crews dressed their currachs every few days, and the litres are ours.
- **Oiled hides and pelts keep three times as long as raw hide** afloat, an estimate: fat dressing sheds water but is no tannage, and brain- and fat-dressed hides wash out and harden when wet ([leather-dictionary.com](https://leather-dictionary.com/index.php/Brain_tanning)), but no keeping time was measured for them.
- **Steering under sail is vanilla's.** Under sail the outrigger and the sailboat turn as vanilla turns its sailboat, at its rate whatever their speed; a rudder or steering paddle whose grip on the water comes from the boat's own way is still to come. After a shunt the outrigger's helmsman stays in his seat, facing the end that was the bow.
- **Sails and masts never break.** The rig's every tension is known, but no mast, spar, line or sail gives under it yet, and none heels the hull: the crab claw's coefficients were measured at 10° of heel, the hull sails upright.
- **The waterline lies level.** A hull doesn't heel yet, so a side crack leaks by how low it sits, not by which side the wind leans it to; heel comes in [Seamanship](Roadmap#seamanship). How fast a hull settles after a crest, 0.3 of critical in heave, is ours too.
- **Cargo weighs only its containers.** A player carries loads no real crew could lift, so what is in a basket or chest adds nothing to a knock; the containers count at their own empty weight, and what lies in a hold counts for nothing. Mail, which the game slows less than its real 10 to 14 kg would, comes out light.
- **The uncut wood backs a side by its depth.** What backs a side below the cut is the solid core across the beam, but the mod counts the depth left uncut, the same as under the bottom. Both are many times the 5 cm wall; the difference shows only on the last hollowings.
