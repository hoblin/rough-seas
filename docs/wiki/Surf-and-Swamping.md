Out on the water, every few seconds a wave reaches your vessel. Most lift it and pass. Some come over the side, and some break on it. What each does depends on the wave, drawn fresh every time from the [sea state](Wind-and-Waves#every-wave-is-different), and on the shape of the hull it meets.

## Shipping water

A wave's crest stands about half its height above the sea. A hull rises with the wave, a light and buoyant one more than a heavy one; what is left of the crest above the hull's sides, its *freeboard*, comes aboard. Higher sides keep more out, and so does a hull that rides over the swell instead of sitting in it.

Boats with a hull hold that water in their [bilge](Bailing). The [raft](Raft) and the [reed boat](Reed-Boat) hold none: the sea washes straight through them, and there is nothing to bail.

## Water aboard

A litre of water weighs a kilogram, and a bilge holds hundreds. As it fills, a boat:

- **rides lower.** Full, it sinks by its whole freeboard, so each wave comes aboard more easily than the last;
- **gets slower.** It paddles as hard and pushes a heavier hull, so the lighter the boat, the more its water slows it: half full, a heavy dugout keeps nine tenths of its speed, a coracle four fifths and a hide currach, whose bilge holds 15 times its weight, two thirds;
- **rolls harder.** The water sloshing across the bilge leans with every roll and adds to it, twice the roll when full, and a breaker half as high overturns it.

## Swamping

A full bilge swamps the vessel. It lies awash, its sides level with the sea: nobody can paddle it or sit in it, but everyone aboard can hold on to it, in the water at its side. Then bail it out, wait for a lull, or leave it and swim. Every wave breaks over a swamped hull and strikes it harder, so in a breaking sea it won't last long.

Wood floats, and so do reed, bark and hide on a wooden frame: a swamped boat stays afloat. Only the [sailboat](Sailboat), heavy with ballast to stand up to its sail, goes to the bottom, throwing everyone off as it founders. Raising a sunk vessel is planned for [1.3](Roadmap#13-the-deep).

## Breakers

A wave breaks when it grows too steep, and a breaking wave is the dangerous one: its crest stands higher, it throws far more water aboard and it strikes the hull like a falling wall.

- **Surf.** Where the water is shallower than about 1.3 times a wave's height, the wave breaks, every time. Real waves do the same: a wave breaks once it is about four fifths as high as the water is deep, which is why the surf line runs further out the higher the sea. In rough weather the last stretch to a beach is all surf.

  ![Where waves break: a big wave breaks further out than a small one, where the water is about 1.3 times its height deep](images/diagrams/surf-line.svg)

- **Whitecaps.** Out in deep water, now and then a wave breaks on its own, more often as the wind rises: about one wave in 50 in a strong breeze (force 6), one in 11 in a gale (force 8), one in 4 in a storm (force 10) and three in four in a hurricane.
- **A swamped vessel.** Once a bilge is full, every wave breaks over it.

A breaker strikes the hull with the energy of the water it throws, which grows very steeply with its height: a 4 m breaker hits about eight times as hard as a 2 m one. A hull full of water takes it harder, up to twice as hard. Each strike is weighed against the hull's [capacity](Hull-Condition#how-a-hull-takes-a-blow), so a [swamped](#swamping) boat in a breaking sea doesn't last.

## Capsizing

> **Switched off for now.** A capsized boat stayed upright on screen while its crew went into the water, with nothing to show what had happened. Capsizing comes back once a vessel is seen to roll over. Until then breakers still ship water and strike the hull, and a full bilge still swamps it.

A breaker as high as a vessel's capsizing height overturns it. Everyone aboard is thrown into the sea, and the bilge fills to the brim. The cargo stays aboard, and the vessel stays afloat, swamped, if it isn't broken. The heights below are for an empty bilge; water aboard [lowers them](#water-aboard), down to half when full.

## How each vessel takes the sea

Measured in the game, unanchored in open water, a few minutes in each sea:

| Waves | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 2 m | The [dugout](Dugout) shipped 232 of its 450 litres, rolling hard. The [outrigger](Outrigger-Canoe) shipped 12 litres and barely rolled. The [currach](Hide-Currach) shipped none. The sea washed over the [raft](Raft). |
| 3 m | The dugout swamped and capsized, and the [bark canoe](Birch-Bark-Canoe) swamped. The [coracle](Coracle) and the raft broke. The outrigger shipped 129 litres; the currach stayed upright. |
| 4 m | The dugout rolled twice as far as the currach and ten times as far as the outrigger. The outrigger swamped and broke on a 5.4 m breaker. |

These were measured before capsizing was switched off, in seas set to each height. A lake raises waves by its size and depth, a [pond barely ripples](Wind-and-Waves#how-high), and rivers have their own danger, [rapids](Rapids): that is where the river boats belong. The currach, the outrigger and the [sailboat](Sailboat) are the vessels for the sea.

## At anchor

A vessel at [anchor](Anchoring) ships less water and takes milder blows: three tenths of each. It still capsizes as easily, because it lies across the waves; turning its bow into the wind comes with sailing in [1.1](Roadmap#11-seamanship).

With [Dangerous oceans](World-Settings#dangerous-oceans) off, waves don't come aboard, strike or capsize anything.
