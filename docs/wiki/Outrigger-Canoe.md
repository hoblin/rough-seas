![The outrigger canoe afloat](images/outrigger-canoe/afloat-bow.webp)

A dugout with a float log held out to one side on three crooked booms lashed across her gunwales and down onto it, and a mast stayed fore, aft and out to the float: 5 blocks long and 3 across, hull, gap and float. For the open sea she takes washstrakes of sawn planks sewn along her sides and across her ends. Her crab-claw sail hangs between two spars, set, reefed or furled to the upper one. Her hull, her float, her poles and her washstrakes can each be of their own wood.

## In Rough Seas

For a long crossing of open sea. The outrigger sails, leaving every rowed boat far astern; for now she sails at vanilla's pace, a little slower than the sailboat, until sailing reads the wind. The float keeps the narrow hull upright where a dugout would roll over. It also stands out on its side, so knocks from that side work its lashings loose rather than cracking her hull; let them give out and the float and rig come away, leaving the dugout. Built without a saw she is a coastal boat whose low sides ship water as a dugout's do; once you have a saw, washstrakes raise her side for the open sea. It is clumsy to turn and it can't be picked up, so where you launch it is where it lives.

## Where it comes from

The outrigger is the boat of the Austronesian expansion, the greatest migration by sea before modern times: from Taiwan and Island Southeast Asia, canoes like it carried people west to Madagascar and east across the Pacific to Hawaii, Rapa Nui and New Zealand. A burnt and hewn dugout hull carries a single float (the *ama*), a smaller log shaped with an adze and pointed at its ends, on booms lashed across the hull with fibre cord, and a crab-claw sail of woven matting on two spars. All of it was made without metal or saw. The model is a small single-outrigger sailing canoe of that family, with linen in place of the matting.

## How it is built

You don't craft an outrigger in the grid: you rig a finished [dugout](Dugout) where it lies, piece by piece, and each piece shows as you lay it. None of it needs a saw.

![The whole build, from the logs to the washstrakes](images/outrigger-canoe/build-outrigger-canoe.webp)

1. **Split the poles.** Lay three logs of one wood on their sides, end to end, anywhere, set a [splitting wedge](Recipes#splitting-wedge) on each, and hold an axe on each wedge in turn until it is driven home. Once the last is home, the trunk splits into 64 poles of 3 m. The outrigger takes six, and a longer pole serves too, cut down. Any wood will do, a modded tree's too.

   ![Three logs with a wedge on each](images/outrigger-canoe/build-poles.webp)

2. **Lay the float.** Lay three more logs of one wood on their sides beside a finished dugout, end to end along it, the middle log beside its middle, a block off: hull, gap, float.

   ![A dugout with three logs alongside](images/outrigger-canoe/build-float.webp)

3. **Join them.** Hold a pole on the dugout. The first boom goes across its gunwales onto the logs, and the logs become the float where they lie, still three logs a metre across. The dugout is now the outrigger: the same hull, with its cracks and the water in it. A float on the far side turns it end for end. A dugout pushed off the block grid, or lying a little askew, is squared onto the logs, so the float lies exactly where they lay; push one lying too far askew straight first.

   ![The first boom across](images/outrigger-canoe/build-boom.webp)

4. **Lay the booms.** Hold the other two poles on the outrigger for the fore and aft booms. Each is a crooked pole that rests on the gunwales and on top of the logs, as the curved booms of Hawaii and the Marshall Islands came down to the float. All the poles must be of one wood.
5. **Hew the float.** Hold an axe on the outrigger. Each pass hews the float all round, top, sides and bottom alike, from the square logs toward a round log 30 cm across with its ends pointed and lifted. Six passes take it down from 3000 litres of wood to 196. The booms settle onto it as it shrinks: they never hang in the air and never pass through wood. While the float is still the logs, the outrigger weighs two tonnes and barely moves.

   ![Three passes hewn, the booms settled onto the float](images/outrigger-canoe/build-hewing.webp)

6. **Lash it.** Hold rope on the outrigger to lash each boom to both gunwales and straight onto the float: thirteen turns a lashing, 52 metres in all, six ropes.

   ![Three booms lashed](images/outrigger-canoe/build-lashed.webp)

7. **Step the mast.** Hold the last three poles on the outrigger: the mast, then the crab claw's upper and lower spars.

   ![The mast and spars up](images/outrigger-canoe/build-mast.webp)

8. **Bend on the sail and set up the stays.** Hold linen cloth on the outrigger, four pieces for its 3.5 m² of sail, then four ropes: the forestay, the backstay, the shroud out to the float and the sheet. The outrigger is finished and takes its seats.

   ![Rigged, the sail furled on its spar](images/outrigger-canoe/build-rigged.webp)

9. **Add washstrakes for the open sea.** Any time after it is finished, once you have a saw, hold planks of one wood on the outrigger: a board along each side four planks long and a board across each end of the hollow, ten in all. Then hold four ropes on it to sew them on, a stitch every 7.6 cm. Sewn, they raise the sides 15 cm and add 422 litres to the hold: a 2 m swell that boards a dugout runs past the outrigger. Without them it is a coastal boat.

   ![Washstrakes sewn along the sides and across the ends](images/outrigger-canoe/build-strakes.webp)

Push it into the water with Shift + right-click and an empty hand. It is heavy and moves a few centimetres a push over dry ground, so rig it at the water's edge. An outrigger from before the build loads finished, with its washstrakes on, as it was built.

## Its neighbours

- The [hide currach](Hide-Currach) is the other small boat for the sea: slower, but it carries cargo and can be carried.
- The [sailboat](Sailboat) sails faster for now and carries far more.
- The [dugout](Dugout) is the same hull without the float: she is built on one, and is as tough, and tippy at sea.
