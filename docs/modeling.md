# Modeling

Read before changing a shape, with `bin/model help`. Shapes are edited in Blender 4.5, headless, through the `phonon/blender-vintagestory-json` add-on. `bin/model` installs the add-on at a pinned commit into the gitignored `.blender/`, applies `bin/lib/vsmodel/addon.patch`, and runs Blender with no window. After changing anything under `bin/lib/vsmodel`, run `bin/model test`.

## Our vessels are built by code

A hull of a few hundred tilted boards is no shape to place by hand, so each vessel of ours has a builder in `bin/lib/hulls/` (Python, no Blender), and `bin/model build [VESSEL...]` writes its shape JSON from it, and into her entity JSON every value the model measures (see [What a build writes](#what-a-build-writes)). The builder is the source: change the vessel there and rebuild; an edit made to the shape or to a generated value alone is lost on the next build. Look at the result with `bin/model preview` and in the game.

`bin/model build` is a generator, not a test. It builds the vessels side by side and prints each one's output as she finishes: under a minute for all of them, the bark canoe the longest. Run it only when what it writes must change, and only for the vessels whose output changes:

- a vessel's own builder changed: `bin/model build VESSEL` (the outrigger is built from the dugout's hull, so a dugout change rebuilds both);
- code every builder uses changed (`shape.py`, `fit.py`, `wounds.py`, `physics.py`, `transform.py`), or the stroke fitting (`stroke.py`, `arm.py`, `leg.py`, `limb.py`, `held.py`): every vessel, and `tying`;
- a value of her data the builders read changed (her `parts`' materials, the materials table, `config/vessels/mass.json`, her seats): that vessel; a change to the bark canoe's model rebuilds every vessel, since `physics.stands` measures the others against her;
- a merge left a generated file in conflict (a shape, `player-*.json`): take either side and rebuild the vessels that file belongs to; never resolve it by hand.

Don't run it to see whether something still works: a refactor, a merge, a review. Read what the change touches first; the checks a build runs (the boxes, the tools' stances) run on the build the change needs, if it needs one. A change to anything else, a comment, a doc or a wiki page, needs none.

- `shape.py` holds the parts, the way vanilla builds its boats: a `Hull` lofted through stations, each station a half-section from keel to sheer, whose strakes are boards nested yaw → pitch → flare like the sailboat's prow chains, with ribs, rails, a transom and the water mask; `beam` and `path` for anything along a line (bundles, logs, booms, oars, stays); `box`, `run` and `group` for the rest. Every face gets an explicit UV at a spot of the texture picked from its element's name, so boards rarely repeat one grain.
- A vessel's bow is at −x: at yaw 0 it moves toward +z, which is model −x, and its front seat sits there. A builder drawn the other way round turns its elements about the hull's middle with `Shape.turn_about`.
- `Hull` is the box round her finished hull, drawn by `shape.hull_box` from the parts the builder names: the mechanics measure her by it. The seats and the outrigger's `OarStorage` are carried over unchanged from the current file by `Shape.keep`: their bounds are what reach and seats measure. The outrigger's halyard cleat (`LowerMastStorage`) and her two sail steps are built with her rig (`fittings`). Her shape draws her rig only while she is being built, as the stages `Mast`, `SailFurled` and `Stays`; once she is rigged, `roughseas:windage` takes those groups out of her mesh (its `drawn`) and draws her mast, shore, spars, lines and cloth itself.
- A rigid hull is boxes laid like boards. Cloth, and anything else that moves (a sail that fills and trims, spars that swing on their lines), is not: `RigRenderer` builds it in code each frame as a mesh of triangles, from the points the builder measures and writes into her entity (`rig.py`, `wind.py`), in her own frame so it heaves with her. Never tile a sail with boxes: a staircase edge, or rags, is what that gives.
- A hollow hull carries `hideWater`, flat faces just under the rim that the `hidewatersurface` behaviour draws into depth before the sea, as vanilla's sailboat does, so the sea doesn't show inside the boat. The entity and item shapes leave it out with `ignoreElements`. The mask is tessellated a few seconds after the vessel appears; a shot taken sooner still shows water inside.
- `coracle.py` also writes `coracle-back.json`: the finished bowl under the backpack step-parent transform the worn shape keeps.
- A hull that holds water carries `Bilge`: eight levels, `Bilge1` to `Bilge8`, from the floor to just under `hideWater`, each sized to the hull at its height. The entity and item leave the group out of their mesh; the bilge draws the one level as full as it is, level with the sea while the hull rolls (`BilgeRenderer`).
- A vessel built in place carries its build stages as groups: `Blank` the whole trunk as it stands, `Char` its charred hollow, `Grass` the tinder, `Wood` the firewood laid on it (`Wood{n}x1` to `x4`), `Fire` the fire once it has caught (`Fire{n}At1` to `At3`, its thirds) and `Clay` its rim, ball by ball in the order the balls cover it, each with one child per hollowing, and `Shell` the finished hull. The dugout's `Blank0` is the bare trunk and `Bark` its bark, `Bark1` to `Bark15` in the order the axe strips them; together they are exactly the five logs, every face mapped as the log block maps it from the log's own faces (`bark`, `rings`). Each level after is drawn as the `Shell` is, boards on a loft worked a sixth of the way from the bare trunk to the hull, so the last hewing gives the `Shell` itself. The loft runs the log row's whole length, its bottom raked up to the log's end, where `Cap` closes it in end grain mapped as the log block maps it. Each end of the hollow is half a bowl (`Bowl{j}x{k}`), built as the coracle's: rings by height, the floor sweeping up into the end in the `Shell` and flat at a level's floor, and staves by angle, their sides lapped into the walls; `Rim` lays strips along the top of the end, from the bowl's mouth out to the hull's side and across beyond it, and the clay goes on as the same strips. A level and the `Shell` are drawn in the sections a hewing works one at a time, cut at the logs' joints so no log's face splits (`Blank{n}x1` to `x3` from the bow, the first and last running on to the log's ends; `Blank{n}Stern` and `Blank{n}Bow` hold the rim and the cap, a naming the builder had before its sections), each holding `Inside`, the floor or bare logs the next hewing's inside work takes, and in the end sections the bowl; `Hewn{n}x{j}` is what that work leaves: the next hewing's floor and bowl, the walls staying as they stood since its inner faces lie no further out, and on the bare trunk a trench, its outer faces mapped as the logs', closed at its ends by bowls as wide as it, up to the trunk's top. Inside it `Step` closes the hewn floor against the next section's char: an upright face in bands, each as wide as the hollow at its height, and before it a lip of strips worked back into the floor, each at its own slope. Hewn wood, floors and the walls' inner faces, is drawn with `hewn`, vanilla's scorched `wood/charred`, since wood scraped down from char is scorched brown. `Char{n}` is drawn per section too, and the clay's balls never cross a section's end. `roughseas:burnout` shows the ones the build is at; the item leaves them out. `dugout.py` draws them and prints the trunk's, the hull's and the hold's litres. A bark canoe framed in place carries `Sheet`, the bark lying flat before her frame goes in, and one group per stage (`Frame`, `Stems`, `Lashing`, `Sheathing`, `Ribs`, `Pitch`) with one child per piece, `Frame1`, `Frame2`…: `roughseas:framing` counts the pieces from them and shows those laid. Her bark is drawn as each frame piece leaves it, `Skin1` to `Skin7` (the `Frame` stage `lifts` `Skin`), and each frame piece wherever it lies, `Frame{n}At{k}`, shown while `k` pieces are laid (the stage `follows` itself), the way Adney's Malecite raised one: the inwales and thwarts lie flat on the sheet as her gunwale frame, the last thwart turns her middle up square around it (`upright`), each knife slash (`Slashes`) turns up the next length out to the sheet's ends (`spans`), the first outwale (`Outwales`) raises the frame to her sheer and her finished bark, the sheet's `surplus` still past each end, and each knife cut (`Ends`) trims one off. `Frame`, `Slashes`, `Outwales` and `Ends` all lift `Skin`, so its levels count their pieces together, `Skin1` to `Skin13`. The sheet is tiled no larger than her skin's boards, so its bark is drawn at the hull's scale; a level that repeats another is a copy of it, UVs and all. Each `Skin` level carries the `depthMetres` she stands at, which the build floats her by. `barkcanoe.py` draws them, and works out the ropes from the lashing's length. The outrigger is drawn on the dugout's own hull (`outrigger.py` imports it from `dugout.py`), with her rigging's stages the same way (`Booms`, `Hewing`, `Lashing`, `Mast`, `SailFurled`, `Stays`, and the optional washstrakes `Strakes` and `Seams`). `FloatLog1` is the three logs as they lay; `Hewing` is `successive`, each pass `Hewing{n}` replacing the one before and the first replacing `FloatLog`, and each boom is drawn resting on every pass, `Booms{n}At{k}`, as `Booms` `follows` `Hewing`. The reed boat's `Blank` is her two bundles' middles and `Flat` their ends as they lie, written as the bundles block's shapes too (`shapes/block/reedbundle-{one,two}.json`), so the bundles become the boat without moving; `Lashing` lashes them and `Tips` replaces `Flat` with the ends bound up, each `Tips{n}` drawn at four sags, `Tips{n}Droop0` dry to `Droop3` sunk, for `roughseas:droop`. The coracle carries `Lattice`, her laths lying flat as bound, `LatticeBent`, the same laths bent up once her gunwale starts, `Binding`, `Gunwale`, `Cover` and `Lacing`; the currach `Gunwale`, `EndLashing`, `Ribs`, `Stringers`, `Cover`, `Lacing`, `Thwart` and `Oars`, drawn the right way up and turned over by her build while she is upside down. Her gunwale is drawn twice, `Gunwale{n}At1` with the stern still open and `Gunwale{n}At2` closed, as `Gunwale` `follows` `EndLashing`. A group may carry `woodLitres` and `waterlineSquareMetres` for each piece, measured by its builder, which the build weighs and floats her by (the outrigger's float). Every builder measures her mass, hold, deck and waterplane and the `swimmingOffsetY` she floats at through `physics.afloat`, and writes them into her entity JSON. `stages()` writes on each piece's group the `metres` of lath or pole it takes, measured along the run its `frame` texture draws; a cover's pieces are its sections of one small hide's area each, cut by `coracle.py` round the bowl and by `currach.py` along her length, and both print the ropes their lashings take and the sections their covers come to.
- The seats (`FrontSeat`, `BackSeat`) and the reed boat's basket cell `Cargo` live only in the JSON, kept by `Shape.keep`: move them there, the seats with room for the pose's legs, and check with `bin/scene seats` and `bin/scene cargo`. A container is drawn from its cell's `from` at its own 0 to 16 voxels and at its true size, so the cell is placed, unscaled, so that the container lies on the centreline with its bottom on what it rests on.
- A vessel with sides carries holds, built by `cargo.py`: the builder names where each hold's piles lie along and across her (`cargo.block`), and `cargo.hold` rests each pile, a third of a block square, on her floor under its inner edge, raised only as far as keeps its outer corners inside her outside, so a pile may sink into her wall but never shows through it. It writes the hold's box (`MidHold`, `AftHold`, `ForeHold` or `Hold`, its attachment point the hold's selection box), and `roughseas:cargo`'s `holds` and `pileScale` into her entity. A floor that rises at her ends lifts the piles there; lay them where she is flat enough, and clear of a thwart lower than a full pile.
- The currach rows with her own oars, laid on her thole pins as her last build stage: `currach.py` describes their motion as a stroke, and `stroke.crew` fits the seated player's arms to it: each hand to a grip element, frame by frame, with the engine's own pose math and the seat's pose from the game, turned the way the seat's `mountRotation` faces it (her rower faces her stern). The build writes the vessel's animations into its shape, the rower's into `patches/player-<name>.json`, and `<temp>/model/<vessel>-rowed.json`, the vessel with the player seated in it, for `bin/model pose`: look at a stroke there before the game, framed on the rower with `bin/model pose ... Rower`.
- Every other stroke is a tool held in the right hand: `held.py` describes it as the tool's path (`paddling` along the side, `sculling` over the bow, `poling` off the bottom), fits both arms to the tool's two grips as `crew` does, then keys the hand's `ItemAnchor` so the game draws the item exactly where the path puts it, through the item's `tpHandTransform` (`EntityShapeRenderer.RenderItem`). That transform turns about the hand point with no origin and no scale, and puts the tool's right-hand grip at the anchor, so one stroke holds every length of pole and vanilla's crude oar alike. A tool that looks the same turned about its grips' line (the square pole, a quarter turn) is held in whichever of those holds the check below finds smoothest against the other tools. The build prints how deep the tool reaches under the water, and each stroke plays at the rate `config/propulsion.json` rows at. `paddling` draws both sides, port mirrored about the seat with the hands on each other's grips, and the change to each, the paddle carried over the bow while the hands slide past each other along it; the item stays in the right hand, its anchor keyed wherever that hand holds. A change starts and ends with the very arms of the ready poses it joins, and a hand moving to a grip it holds the other way turns over as it slides. The build writes how far out from her middle the blade drives: the vessel's `paddleAbeamMetres`. Each vessel's builder writes her rower's strokes, `raft.py` vanilla raft's, and `<temp>/model/<vessel>-<tool>.json`, the vessel with the rower and the tool in it, for `bin/model pose ... Rower`.
- A pole is worked standing wherever `physics.stands` finds her hull bears a man standing (`docs/wiki/The-Science-of-a-Body-in-the-Water.md`), and from the seat where it doesn't. `held.standing_poling` stands the rower in `roughseas-stand` on her floor at the point the builder gives, his origin at his soles, and keys his hips and legs each frame: `leg.py` plants each foot on the floor and prints a foot off it or a knee or hip past its range. The pole is laid from his upper hand to its foot on the bed a metre down, planted at the working angle `config/propulsion.json` counts its reach at, and his hands slide along it, `ToolRight` and `ToolLeft` moved along the tool by the stroke: they walk together up it through the shove and it slides forward through them on the recovery. `PolePath` gives both ways from one cycle: ahead the foot is planted astern of his hands, astern ahead of them, his hands crossing over on the recovery so the upper one stays aft; every stroke starts and ends halfway through the recovery, the pole upright, which is the ready pose, so either blends into the other. A looping stroke that starts where another already set the arm takes that arm there (`fit`). The build writes where his hands plant it each way and the pole and bed it drew (`plantAheadMetres`, `plantAsternMetres`, `plantAcrossMetres`, `drawnPoleMetres`, `drawnBedMetres`): the game sounds the bed at the foot and slides the drawn pole to it. How far from the seat's attachment point he stands and how high his hands end the shove are the stance's `riderOffset` and `handsAboveWaterMetres`: the build writes them into her pole config (her entity JSON, or `propulsion-vessels.json` for a vanilla boat) and leaves the rest of the file as it is.
- A held tool never passes through her hull: `held()` checks each key against the walls the builder names, and `rested()` moves a tool inside them the shortest way out, outboard or up, before the hands are fitted to it. The build prints how far the hull holds it out. That is a stopgap over strokes whose lines are guessed per vessel; #252 has the strokes take their path from the hull instead, until no vessel prints the line.
- The game draws an animation by lerping each Euler angle from key to key, and blends one animation into the next the same way, channel by channel. So an orientation written in the wrong one of its Euler forms swings the long way round, a paddle spinning in a quarter of a second (#269). `motion.py` writes every rower's turns in forms that turn the short way across the switches the game makes, read from the vessel's `strokes` as `MountStrokes` plays them, and the build refuses a vessel any of whose turns would still swing the long way round, naming the switch, the element, the degrees and the frame. A refusal names a real glitch: two strokes that meet in different poses, or a loop of switches no writing can close. Fix the strokes, never the check.
- The arms are anatomy (`arm.py`), and know nothing of oars. A hand holds each grip one way in every stroke, the way that strains it least over all. The hand lands on its grip exactly; what is left free is how far the elbow swings round the line from shoulder to hand, and the arm takes the swing a body would: the shoulder, elbow and wrist inside their measured ranges, the fist closed across the gear's shaft (its x), the arm outside the torso, joint rotations away from the ends of their range, and the arm's own weight carried with the least effort. The build prints how near the hands come to their grips (a miss means the stroke asks more than the arms reach) and every joint pushed past its range or arm through the body, with the frame; move the stroke, not the arm, until it prints none.
- `tying.py` builds no vessel: it writes the knot a standing player ties wherever rope or cord goes into a vessel, `patches/player-tying.json`. It describes the knot as the two hands' paths and fits the arms to them as `crew` does; the first-person twin is the same knot tied further out and higher, where the first-person view shows it. `bin/model build tying` rewrites it.
- In an animation of version 0 an offset is turned by the element's rotation; `stroke.moved` puts an element at a point in the shape, turned as asked, from where it rests.
- `wounds.py` draws a cracking hull's damage into its model, last in a builder, once the `Hull` box is kept: `wound(shape, levels, lining, framing)` puts, on each level of skin a build shows (`""` the finished hull, `Blank3`, `Skin5`…), a `Wound{Place}{level}` group beside the board nearest each place's middle with room for the wound, in that board's frame, holding the crack at each stage, `Scratched`, `Cracked`, `Split`, `StoveIn`. Each runs along the grain through the wall, or a solid log's face, and through any `lining` inside it: a split or a place stove in as long as the shell's `spanMetres` from the entity JSON, a scratch and a crack a share of it, none shorter than 1.5 voxels; a group beside the board, never its child, keeps every element with children a zero-size group. A `framing`, the stage of ribs or laths under the skin with their texture, breaks its member nearest each place from a split on: the member is wrapped whole in `Wound{Place}Whole`, and its two halves, turned inward about their far ends, sit beside it in `Wound{Place}Frame`. Port places sit a little toward the bow and starboard toward the stern, as `HullShape.PointOf` has them. The outrigger draws her float's forms as its lashings give, `FloatLashing{Loose,Torn,Shifted}`, each listing the elements it `replaces`. Look at them in the game, struck with `/bridge strike`: a preview draws every stage at once.
- `bin/damage` draws the overlays a hull without wounds shows, wood's (vanilla's raft and sailboat) and reed's, four stages each, from a seed; `bin/damage PREVIEW.png` lays them over their textures to look at.

## Writing a builder

- **Compute once what a loop doesn't change.** Pose what stays fixed outside the loop over what varies, such as the trunk in a frame or the stroke's constants, and share it between the alternatives tried.
- **Build a part once.** Reuse a stage already built; don't call its builder again for a report or a second shape.
- **Reject cheaply first.** Test a box's bounds before a point's depth in it.
- **Hot math goes through `transform.py`.** Its helpers are unrolled for speed; a loop that runs a million times takes no generators or `sum()` over three or four terms.
- **A new mechanic is a module, called by its name.** A builder writes `import cargo` and `cargo.hold(...)`, never `from cargo import hold`, and keeps what it builds for that mechanic inside its own functions. A name imported or defined at a builder's top level replaces any it already has, with no error: the holds' `floor_at` once replaced the dugout's hewing floor and laid her tinder inside the log.
- **A fact lives once.** A measured quantity goes in `physics.py` with its source. The voxel is defined once, in `RoughSeas/Units/voxel.json`, and `units.py` converts by it; never write 16 or 1/16. Import it; never copy it into a builder.
- **A builder's geometry is voxels.** Its own coordinates and sizes carry no unit; whatever crosses into another module (`physics.afloat`, `stands`, the strokes) or out into a file carries its unit in its name.
- **Measure before optimising:** `python3 -m cProfile -s cumtime bin/lib/hulls/VESSEL.py OUT`.
- **Write a file another vessel reads with `shape.save`.** The vessels build side by side.

## What a build writes

What the model can measure is never typed into JSON: the build writes it, and a build rewrites it.

- `entity.record(vessel, values)` writes the values into her entity JSON (`entities/vessel/<vessel>.json`), or, for vanilla's raft and sailboat, into the patch that gives her each behaviour. It edits the file's text in place (`jsontext.put`): every other character stays as it was, and a missing key is added after its partner or last in its object. Each object it writes opens with a comment naming what it holds: `// Generated by `bin/model build` from the model; a build rewrites these values: ...`. The game's JSON reader skips the comment; the builders read these files through `jsontext.load`, which does too.
- A length, area or volume is written twice, in metres (a volume in litres) and in voxels, the unit in each name (`units.both`: `depthMetres` and `depthVoxels`, `capacityLitres` and `capacityCubicVoxels`). The mod reads the metres, so nothing converts a static value at runtime; the runtime converter, `RoughSeas.Units.Voxels`, is for what is computed on the fly, such as a point struck on the hull. Vanilla's own keys (`hitboxSize`, `collisionBoxes`, `riderOffset`) stay in metres alone.
- What is written: her `weight`, her `parts`' shares, `swimmingOffsetY`, `hitboxSize` and collision boxes (the hull box from her origin up, in squares as wide as she is); the hull box (`hullBoxMetres`), depth, waterplane, the bilge's hold, deck and water levels; her shell's backing shares (`wounds.backed` samples her skin and finds the frame lying against it), its spans and its frame's spacing; her windage, side and front, above her waterline with one aboard, its centre, and the centre of her side below it (`wind.py`); a sailing vessel's sail area, set and reefed, and her rig's points, spars and lines (`rig.py` for the crab claw); the paddle's reach and sweep, the oars and the pole's stance; and her build stages' settings (the dugout's burnout, with her hull box round the trunk at each hewing, the outrigger's rigging, the bark canoe's sheet, the lattice layouts).
- What stays hand-written is what the model can't know: materials, real-world sources, seaworthiness from real craft (`heave`, the capsizing breaker as a share of her beam or length, the roll and pitch response), work times, the strength of materials.
- `raft.py` measures vanilla's raft from vanilla's shape (her box round her logs, a log's section, the longest stretch between her lashings) and `sailboat.py` the sailboat from her planking (her box, hold, deck, windage and mast) and her square sail from vanilla's own yard, cloth, sheets and stays; both write into our patches.
- A builder that reads another vessel's entity JSON reads what that vessel's last build wrote: `physics.stands` measures every hull against the bark canoe, so a change to her model takes a second build for the others.

## Boxes inside the round they build

A cuboid that builds a round shape keeps every corner inside the surface it follows, and two faces never share a plane. A corner past the curve shows as a square tooth on the outline; two faces in one plane fight over the pixel and flicker.

- Each builder states the surface every part follows, `shape.within(surface, *parts, only=…)`, and the parts each stage of its build shows together, `shape.together(*parts)`. `Shape.write` checks both and writes nothing on a fault: it names each box and how far its worst posed corner stands beyond its surface, and each two faces in one plane with the area they share. Both measure to 0.01 voxel; `APART`, 0.02 voxel, keeps two faces told apart.
- The surfaces are in `fit.py`: `Oval`, an elliptic bowl whose axes go by height (the coracle); `Loft`, a hull's half-sections, measured along their normal (`Hull.outline`, inset for what lies inside the skin); `Swept`, a solid by its half-breadth at each height (the dugout's hewing passes); `Tube`, an ellipse round a beam's axis between its ends; and a `Union` of them (bundles, the float, its lashings).
- A round section of boxes is two crossed ones, the second `ROUNDED` as wide as the first is high, every corner on the ellipse (`section`, a `beam`'s `rounded`): its half-sides are `w/√(1+k²)` and `k·w/√(1+k²)`, the inscribed square's `r/√2` at `k = 1`. A round thing weighs and floats by its round, never its boxes: the reed bundle's girth and the float's wood come from a circle's area.
- A ring of boards with their thickness inward meets at the outer vertex, so it needs no overlap. A board from a smaller ring to a larger one is as long as the larger chord, and sits in until its corners lie inside (the coracle's sides).
- `Hull.strakes` covers each panel with a board on its lower edge and, where it tapers, one hanging from its upper, each as tall as the panel's narrow end; stations are added where the two can't cover a panel. A board reaches past a station as far as the station's line leans out, unless the next panel runs on in line with it, stops at the hull's ends, and sits in until its outermost corner touches the loft (`seated`); its ends keep clear of every end already cut that faces the same way and overlaps it (`clear_of`), an end inside the hull going on past such a cut into the next board, which starts as far on, so no slit opens through the skin; and its depth keeps clear of its neighbours'. A part laid against the skin from inside, the bark canoe's sheathing, is seated against its boards as they lie (`strakes(clear=...)`), not against the loft, since boards sit in deeper where a panel twists.
- What lies inside another part is placed against it: `seated` sinks it along its normal, `rested` moves it toward the surface's nearest point (a frame resting on bark not yet raised), `widest` finds how far a thwart reaches.
- In a run of beams (`path`) every other one is a little thinner, so neighbours share no plane where they meet; a run continuing a beam already drawn starts thinner.
- Two parts that meet but never pass into each other, a frame on its bark, ribs on the sheathing, are stated with `shape.apart(first, second, pressed=…)`: `Shape.write` refuses a box of the first that passes into one of the second deeper than `pressed`, the depth to which flat boards pressed against a curve pinch the part they lie on. Parts meant to cross (a thwart mortised through its inwales, a lashing round its gunwale) are left out of it.
- What lies inside another part (laths, ties, ribs, thwarts, a lashing round the float) is checked against that part's inside; what stands on its own (lashing turns round a gunwale, stitches, pitch, square poles) states no surface, but the stage it shows in still keeps its faces apart. Wounds go through the skin on purpose and are drawn after the check.

## What bin/model guarantees

- A shape that goes through Blender untouched comes back identical: every value, every animation. `bin/model check` proves it for a shape; `bin/model test` for the probe (`bin/lib/vsmodel/test/probe.json`), the vanilla boats and a fish. The probe holds every feature our builders write, so our own shapes are not in the test; a builder that starts writing a new feature adds it to the probe.
- Blender plays every animation as the game will, on every frame, within 0.1° and 1/800 block. `bin/lib/vsmodel/pose.py` evaluates poses with the engine's own math (`ShapeElement.GetLocalTransformMatrix`, both animation versions, the keyframe resolution of `Animation` and the keyframe interpolation of `ClientAnimator`); `check` compares Blender's evaluated pose with it, and export refuses an animation that would play otherwise.
- What changed in Blender is exported, and only that. Vanilla shapes in the game install are never written.

## What the add-on gets wrong, and what bin/model does about it

- **Disabled faces and faceless elements** (`Hull`, seat boxes): the add-on makes every element a full cube with six textured faces. In Blender those faces carry the material `vs_disabled`; on export the original faces come back.
- **Values it drops**: `windMode`, `windData`, `autoUv`, `renderInEditor`, `scaleX/Y/Z`, the `editor` block. They come back from the original. Scale does not reach Blender: a scaled element (the coracle's carried `Bowl`) shows unscaled there.
- **Texture paths and sizes**: it writes the texture code as the path, and takes the first image's size, so UVs come out scaled when an image is larger than `textureWidth`. bin/model exports with the shape's own size and keeps its paths. A new material's texture is written as the asset its image file is (`game:block/...`); a code the shape does not define gets no entry.
- **Faces without `uv`**: the engine maps them by the element's size, the add-on by the whole texture. bin/model hands the add-on the engine's UVs.
- **Zero-thickness elements** (sails, `hideWater`, plaques; add-on issue #17): it writes placeholder `#0` faces and wrong UV rotations. A plane's faces always come back from the original; only its geometry is editable.
- **Float noise and equivalent rewrites** (`-1.9` as `-1.899999976`, `rotationY` 180 as -180, a UV flipped and turned 180°): values within 0.001 of the original, compared as the engine draws them, keep the original's spelling. Element and attachment point order is kept too.
- **JSON5 shapes** (unquoted keys, comments): read with the pinned `json5` package; strict JSON goes to the add-on and comes back out.
- **Bugs patched in `addon.patch`**: armature import copied `XZY` Euler angles into `XYZ` pose bones, turning every element rotated about two or three axes; a missing texture image crashed the import (a float image size); the test that a bone's object sits on the bone used an absolute 1e-5 on translations up to a hundred units, so float32 noise renamed elements to `b_<name>`.
- **Animations**: the add-on's keyframe conversion is lost by up to 28° (issue #19), so bin/model converts animations itself (`bin/lib/vsmodel/animation.py`). A bone rests at its element's rotation origin, turned by the element's rotation; for an animation of version 0 a pose bone carries the rotation `R′ = R_e⁻¹·R(e+kf)` and the location `R′·(scale·stretch·offset)`, for version 1 `R(kf)` and `scale·(d+offset) − scale·stretch·R(kf)·d` with `d = from − rotationOrigin`. Between keyframes the game interpolates Euler angles and offsets, Blender its own channels; both directions add keys wherever the two would differ by more than 0.005° or 0.001 units, so they play alike on every frame. A spin across ±180° is exported with `rotShortestDistance`, with keys close enough that each segment turns less than 180°, and with a key at every frame any other element is keyed: there the game takes each element's plain lerp, without the shortest path.

## Limits that stay

- The game runs every animation of an entity at the highest `version` among those playing, so a shape keeps one version: new and changed animations are exported in the version the shape already uses (0 for every vanilla shape).
- An element's `scale` does not reach Blender, so the motion of elements below a scaled one cannot be shown there; `check` names them. A scaled element's own motion is exact for a uniform scale. An animation's non-uniform `stretch` combined with rotation has no exact Blender form; uniform stretch is exact.
- A face added in Blender to a side the original lacks is exported; a plane's faces always come from the original.
- **Renaming** an element or attachment point makes it new: the original's extras (disabled faces, wind, scale) do not follow it, and entity JSON that names it (selection boxes, `stepParentTo`, vanilla's `SailUnfurled`/`SailHalf`/`SailFurled`, `ORIGIN/hideWater`) breaks.
- **Cuboids only**: a mesh that is not 8 vertices and 6 faces is skipped. What must bend or move, a sail's cloth, is a mesh the mod builds in code, never a shape element ([Our vessels are built by code](#our-vessels-are-built-by-code)). Rotate in object mode; vertices rotated in edit mode export wrong.
- **Bones without cubes** are not exported; give each bone a cube (a faceless one will do).

## Trying a vanilla shape in the game

A shape under `RoughSeas/assets/game/` does not replace vanilla's on the bench: `bin/client` adds our assets as an origin, and vanilla's `survival` origin loads after it and wins (`AssetManager.GetAssetsDontLoad`). A packaged mod loads after vanilla. To see an edited vanilla shape on the bench, copy it into our domain, prefix its texture paths with `game:`, and point the entity at it with a patch:

```json
[{ "op": "replace", "path": "/client/shape/base", "value": "roughseas:entity/test/sailed", "file": "game:entities/nonliving/boat-sailed.json", "side": "Server" }]
```
