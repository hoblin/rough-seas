4.0 "Shipwright": you build every new vessel where it lies, and move it by paddle, pole and oars, by the rower's own strength.

  • Vessels built in place. Burn out and hew a dugout from a log, with a real fire, hewing you can see and clay. Strip a bark canoe off a standing tree and frame it where it falls. Bind a reed boat from two bundles of dried reeds. Lay laths for a coracle or a currach and cover them with hides that rot unless prepared. Rig an outrigger on a finished dugout. More: Building a vessel.
  • Poles, laths and wedges. Split a row of logs into poles with wedges, blow by blow, and the poles into laths; soak the laths so they bend. More: Recipes.
  • Paddle, pole and oars, by the rower. Speed comes from the rower's strength and the hull. Paddle on both sides, changing sides as the boat veers. Pole standing, where the hull bears you, as far as the pole reaches the bottom. Row the currach facing its stern, on its thole pins. Paddle the raft kneeling. More: Paddles, poles and oars.
  • Hulls you can see. Cracks show on the hull where they are. A blow on a framed hull lands on its skin and the frame behind. The outrigger's float rides on lashings of its own. More: Hull condition.
  • Afloat by mass. A vessel heaves by its own mass, and a rider's weight comes aboard as they sit down. More: The science of the hull.
  • Launching on foot. Push a heavy vessel into the water yourself. More: Building a vessel.
  • Under a crest. Your eye goes under a crest that passes over it, and you hold your breath by the sea you see, on a server too. Both are the work of our first contributor, rhino1998. More: Reading the sea.
